How much deposit to take on a custom order

A custom order deposit should at least cover the materials you buy for it. Then bill the rest in stages, so the money you hold keeps up with the work you have done. Many makers take 50% up front. In the worked example below, a $480.00 quilt with $142.50 of materials needs a deposit of at least 30%, and a 50/50 schedule still leaves the maker $182.50 out of pocket on the finished piece, until a payment on completion closes the gap.

The short answer: a floor, then a schedule

Two decisions make up a deposit policy:

  • The deposit floor: the smallest deposit that pays for the materials you buy before you start. Below it, you fund the order yourself.
  • The payment schedule: when the rest is paid. A schedule with a payment at each stage keeps the money you hold close to what you have spent.

To price the order itself first, see how to price custom orders; this guide starts once you have the price.

1. The deposit floor: materials cost ÷ price

deposit floor = materials bought for the order ÷ order price

Round the percentage up to a whole number, so the deposit still covers the materials. Payments company Stripe gives the same reason for deposits in general: "the deposit should cover these costs so you're not paying out of pocket", and calls 20%–50% of the total "often a good range" (updated Feb 2025). That is general business guidance, not a rule for makers.

2. Common schedules makers use

These are practices sellers describe, not rules:

  • 50% up front, 50% on completion or delivery. The most common answer from makers on a seller forum, often with the deposit non-refundable and "don't ship until balance is paid" (Square Community, Aug 2023).
  • Thirds, or 50% to start, 40% when finished, 10% on delivery. Both are described by small custom woodworking shops; bigger jobs get more, smaller stages (WOODWEB, 2006: old and trade-specific).
  • Start, midpoint and completion. Stripe lists this as one way to treat a deposit as the first payment of a schedule.

An art commission calculator also offers 50/50, 40/20/40, 30/30/40 and 60/40 presets and suggests securing a 40-50% deposit before starting (MoMAA). Treat these as rules of thumb.

Worked example: a $480 custom quilt

Illustrative figures only; every input is invented. Price $480.00. Materials bought at the start: $142.50. Making time 14 hours at an hourly rate of $20 (see how to work out your hourly rate) = $280.00.

The cost of the finished quilt is $142.50 + $280.00 = $422.50, so the margin is $57.50. The deposit floor is $142.50 ÷ $480.00 = 29.69%; rounded up to 30%, the deposit is $144.00, which covers the $142.50 of materials. Rounding the percentage up is our choice, so the deposit never falls short.

3. What you are out of pocket at each stage

At each stage, compare what the buyer has paid with what you have spent, counting your time at your hourly rate. Spent so far: $142.50 once the materials are bought, $282.50 when the quilt is half made (7 hours), and $422.50 when it is finished but not yet delivered. A negative number is what you are out of pocket at that point.

A 30% deposit covers the materials and nothing else: on the finished quilt you have funded $278.50 of your own time. A 50/50 split is better at the start but still leaves you $182.50 out of pocket on a finished piece. Thirds keep you ahead until the work is finished. A payment when the piece is finished (schedule D) puts you $9.50 ahead before it ships, with only the last 10% left on delivery.

4. If an order stops partway

The table also shows what is at stake if a buyer cancels or disappears. Halfway through the quilt, schedule C leaves you $138.50 short; schedule B leaves you $37.50 ahead. What you may keep, and what you must refund, depends on what you and the buyer agreed and on the rules where you sell: check your agreement. Write down the deposit, the stages, what happens if either side cancels, and when the balance is due, before you buy materials.

6. Collect the balance before you ship

Makers on seller forums commonly say: do not ship until the balance is paid and has cleared. Once the piece is gone, the final payment is the only one you cannot hold back. For holiday orders, set the stage dates against your custom Christmas order cut-off, so the last payment arrives before your shipping deadline.

Common questions

How much should I charge for custom orders? Price the order first, from the normal price plus the extra time and materials (how to price custom orders); then set the deposit at no less than its materials cost, rounded up to a whole percent.

Should I take a deposit on custom orders? If you buy materials or spend time that only fits this buyer, yes: otherwise you fund the order yourself, as the paid-on-delivery column shows.

How do I take a deposit? Send an invoice or payment request for the deposit before you start, and a second one for each later stage. How you do that depends on the tools you sell with.

What payment schedule works for big custom pieces? Some makers use thirds, or 50% to start, 40% when finished and 10% on delivery, so each stage is paid before the next starts. See schedules B and D above.

Is a deposit refundable if the customer cancels? It depends on what you agreed. Write it down before you start, and check your agreement and the rules where you sell.
