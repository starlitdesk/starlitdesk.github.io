How to keep track of sales and expenses

To keep track of sales and expenses, write every sale and every cost into one log in the same week it happens: one row each, with the date, where it happened, what it was, the amount and a category. Keep every receipt in one folder, pay business costs from one card or account, and total the log once a month. In the worked example below, one week of a small candle business is nine rows: $317.50 of sales, $12.57 of fees and $142.44 of costs, which leaves $162.49 in the week.

1. One log, one row per sale or cost

The point of a log is that nothing lives only in your head, your inbox or a pile of paper. Every sale and every cost gets its own row, in one place, with the same few columns:

  • Date the money moved.
  • Channel for sales: your online shop, a named fair or market, a wholesale shop, a commission.
  • What it was: the items sold, or what you bought.
  • Amount: what the buyer paid, including any shipping they paid, or what you paid.
  • Fee for sales: the selling or card fee, taken from the payout statement or the card reader app, not from a rate you remember.
  • Category for costs.

Keep sales and costs apart (two lists, or one list with a type column) so that totals per channel and per category come straight out of the log.

2. Pick your cost categories once

Categories let you see where the money goes. Choose them once and keep them, so months can be compared. Craftybase, which makes inventory software for makers, lists categories such as "Supply and materials purchases", "Platform and transaction fees", "Advertising costs", "Equipment" and "Software subscriptions", and names "Packaging and shipping supplies" among the costs sellers most often miss (Craftybase). For a small handmade business, these eight are usually enough: materials, packaging, postage, fees, booth or event, software, ads, tools.

3. Receipts: one folder, the same day

Every cost row should have a receipt behind it. Craftybase's advice is short: "Keep receipts for everything", and "For online purchases, save email receipts in a dedicated folder." For paper receipts, take a photo the day you get them and put it in the same folder, named with the date and amount, so a row in the log and its receipt can be matched in seconds.

4. Keep business money apart

Craftybase also suggests: "Use a dedicated bank account and/or credit card for business expenses." With one card for the business, the card statement is a second list of costs you can check the log against, and personal spending never has to be picked out of it. This is about keeping records you can check; what kind of account you need is a question for your bank and the rules where you live.

5. The weekly routine

A log only works if it is kept up. TheCraftMap, a craft-business blog, puts the routine in one line: "Fifteen minutes every Sunday to log the week's sales and material purchases keeps the whole thing accurate" (TheCraftMap). For fairs it suggests logging sales the night of each fair, "while the receipts and memory are fresh". A weekly session can follow the same order every time:

  1. Copy the week's online sales and fees from your payout statements.
  2. Add fair and market sales: card sales from the card reader app, cash sales from your notes.
  3. Add every cost, one row per receipt in the folder.
  4. Compare the costs with your business card statement, so nothing is missing.
  5. Total the week.

6. Once a month: from the log to the profit check

At the end of the month, the log gives you every number the monthly profit check needs: sales per channel, fees, postage paid, materials bought and running costs per category. Common questions

What is the easiest way to keep track of sales and expenses? One log with one row per sale or cost, filled in once a week, with receipts in one folder. A paper notebook works; a spreadsheet adds the totals for you.

Can I keep track of sales and expenses in a spreadsheet? Yes. Two sheets, one for sales and one for costs, with the columns above and a total per channel and per category, are enough for most small handmade businesses.

Should I log each sale or one total per day? Online, log each order, because each has its own fee. At a fair, one row for card sales and one for cash sales per day is enough, as in the example, as long as the card total matches the card reader app.

How long should I keep receipts? That depends on the rules where you live; this guide does not cover it.
