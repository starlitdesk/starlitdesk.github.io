How to work out your hourly rate for handmade work: pay for the hours you are not making

To work out your hourly rate for handmade work, divide the yearly pay you want from your craft by the hours you actually spend making things, not by all the hours you work. Messages, photos, listings, packing, buying supplies and markets all take time that no buyer pays for directly, so your making hours have to pay for them. If you want $24,000 a year, work 25 hours a week for 46 weeks and spend 60% of that time making, your rate is $24,000 ÷ 690 hours = $34.78 an hour, not the $20.87 you get by dividing by all 1,150 hours. This guide shows each step and how to check your current prices against the result.

1. Choose the yearly pay you want

Start with what you want the craft to pay you in a year: a full income, a part-time income, or a fixed amount on top of another job. Decide whether that figure is before or after tax, and stay with that choice; this guide does not calculate tax. Keep business costs out of this number: materials, fees and overhead are added per item when you price, so they do not belong in your pay.

2. Count the hours you will really work

Take the hours a week you can give the business and multiply by the weeks you will work in a year, after holidays, illness and quiet weeks. Be realistic: an estimate you cannot keep up only makes your rate look lower than it is.

working hours a year = hours a week × working weeks

3. Find your making share

For two typical weeks, write down what you spend each hour on: making, or everything else (messages, photos, listings, social media, packing, post office trips, buying supplies, bookkeeping, markets). The share spent making is your making share. Many sellers are surprised at how low it is.

making hours = working hours × making share
hourly rate = yearly pay ÷ making hours

This is the rate to use in the labour line when you price a product: the time you measure with a timer while making it.

If you price with $20.87 an hour, you are only paid for the hours you make things. The 460 hours of admin, photos and packing are unpaid, and the year ends well short of $24,000.

4. Check what your prices pay you now

Turn it round: start from a price you charge today and see what it pays per making hour.

earn per making hour = (price − fees − materials − packaging − overhead) ÷ making hours per item

Take a lined zip pouch sold for $31, with 40 minutes of making, $4.95 of materials, $0.60 packaging, $0.50 overhead and an example fee of 9.5% + $0.45 per order ($3.40 at this price). It leaves $21.56 for 40 minutes: $32.33 per making hour. At a 60% making share, that is $19.40 for every hour you work, a little under the $20.87 per working hour that $24,000 needs.

Priced from the $34.78 rate, the pouch costs $29.24 with your time, and the price that pays your rate after fees is $32.80. At $33, your pay is covered but the business keeps only $0.18. To also keep a 30% margin for slow months, new tools and growth, the formula from our guide to pricing handmade items gives $49.07.

5. Check the volume you need

A rate only works if you can sell the hours. 690 making hours at 40 minutes a pouch is 1,035 pouches a year, about 22 a week. If you cannot sell that many, the target, the hours or the prices have to change.

6. If the rate looks too high

You have real options, and you can mix them:

  • Raise your making share. Batch photos and listings, pack once a day, reuse templates for messages. In the example, moving from 60% to 70% making time lowers the rate needed from $34.78 to $29.81.
  • Make faster, not cheaper. Batching cutting and finishing, simpler designs or better tools lower the making time per item.
  • Raise prices where buyers see the value, and explain the materials and work involved.
  • Lower the target consciously for a hobby-sized business, knowing the real number.

7. Overhead: in the rate or per item, never both

Some sellers add their yearly fixed costs (tools, software, studio rent) to the pay target and leave overhead out of item costs. Others, like the formula in our pricing guides, add a small overhead amount to each item. Either works. Doing both charges the same costs twice, and doing neither leaves them unpaid.

8. Re-check once or twice a year

Your making share, hours and target change. Keep a week of time notes each season and re-run the numbers; then update the rate in your pricing.
