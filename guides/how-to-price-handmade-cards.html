How much should I charge for handmade cards?

To price a handmade card, add up the materials (about $1 for a layered card in our example), pay your minutes at an hourly rate, add the card payment fee, then add a margin. In the worked example a 15-minute one-off card costs $6.09 to make and needs $9.26 for a 30% margin. The same design made in a batch of 12, at 5 minutes each, costs $2.76, so a $5 or $6 price pays. Check any "3 for $10" deal first: at batch cost it keeps $1.31 for three cards.

The short answer: time decides the price

Paper, an envelope and a sleeve cost cents. Your minutes cost dollars. That is why the same card can be well priced at $5 when you make twelve of one design, and lose money at $5 when you make it once. Three numbers answer the question for your own cards:

  • True cost: materials plus your time per card.
  • Break-even price: the price at which a sale pays that cost and the payment fee, and nothing more.
  • Your price: break-even plus the margin you want to keep.

Step 1: cost one card

Work from pack prices. One 8.5 × 11 in sheet of cardstock folds into two A2 cards (4.25 × 5.5 in), so each card base costs half a sheet. Divide envelope and sleeve packs the same way. Then time yourself, from cutting to sleeved, and pay those minutes at your hourly rate (see how to work out your hourly rate).

Pack prices are one retailer's US list prices, read Fri 16 Oct 2026: 110 lb white cardstock, 250 sheets for $24.19 (retailer page), so $0.05 a card; A2 envelopes, 250 for $50.89 (retailer page), so $0.20; A2 cello sleeves, 100 for $33.99 (retailer page), so $0.34. Prices move; use your own. Embellishments, ink and adhesive are invented figures.

Step 2: find your break-even price after fees

A card reader charges a percentage of each payment plus a fixed amount. The example uses 2.6% + $0.15 per in-person card payment, the rate Square's US fee page lists first for tap, dip or swipe (we read that first column as its free plan; read Fri 16 Oct 2026). Use your own provider's rate.

break-even price = (true cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee %)
price for a target margin = (true cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − margin %)

One-off card: break-even (6.09 + 0.15) ÷ 0.974 = $6.41; for a 30% margin (6.09 + 0.15) ÷ 0.674 = $9.26. Batch card: break-even (2.76 + 0.15) ÷ 0.974 = $2.99; for a 30% margin (2.76 + 0.15) ÷ 0.674 = $4.32.

Step 3: test the usual single-card prices

The same vendor guide gives "$3 to $5 each" for simple stamped or printed cards, "$6 to $10 each" for hand-painted or watercolor cards and "$7 to $12 each" for detailed or mixed-media cards. Here is what $5 and $6 keep:

Fee = price × 2.6%, rounded to the cent, + $0.15. Keeps = price − fee − true cost ($6.09 one-off, $2.76 batch).

At $5.00 the one-off card loses $1.37 once your time is paid. Batched, the same price keeps $1.96. If a design takes you 15 minutes and you cannot batch it, price it like the painted or mixed-media cards, around $9.26 or more.

For a 30% margin a pack needs (cards × 2.76 + 0.15) ÷ 0.674: $20.70 for 5 and $41.17 for 10. A one-off card in a "3 for $10" deal loses $8.68 on the three. Christmas cards: batch the design, price the pack

Christmas cards suit batching: one design, many copies, sold in packs. Plan the hours before you plan the price. 60 cards of one design at 5 minutes each take 5 hours and cost $165.60 at $2.76 a card. Sold as six packs of 10 at $42, they keep 6 × $13.16 = $78.96 after fees and after paying your time. Buying sleeves by the 1,000 ($209.99, so $0.21 each: retailer page) saves $0.13 a card, so the batch card costs $2.63, if you will use that many.

Christmas Day 2026 is a Friday, and Black Friday is Friday 27 November. Count card hours against everything else you make: how much holiday stock to make. Taking personalised orders? See when to stop taking custom Christmas orders. Planning a sale price? Check it with Black Friday discount maths.

Selling one card online and posting it

Online, the payment fee and the stamp change the sum. With an online card fee of 2.9% + $0.30 (Stripe's standard US rate, read Fri 16 Oct 2026) the fee on $6.00 is $0.47. A US First-Class Forever stamp is $0.82 since Sunday 12 July 2026, up from 78 cents (USPS, effective date confirmed by a university mail notice). If you post a batch card in its own envelope with one stamp and charge nothing extra for postage, $6.00 keeps 6.00 − 0.47 − 0.82 − 2.76 = $1.95 (32.50%). Square and thick cards cost more to post; check USPS before you set a price. For postage on larger orders, see how much to charge for shipping.

Rules of thumb you will see

An old card-making forum thread (Apr 2011, Splitcoaststampers) shows crafters pricing at $1.75 to $5 and using 1.5 to 3 times materials. For the example: 1.5 × 1.09 = $1.64 and 3 × 1.09 = $3.27. Even $3.27 keeps only $0.27 on a batch card after a $0.24 fee, and both are far below the one-off card's $6.09 cost. A multiple of materials ignores your time, so use it as a floor test, not as the answer.

Common questions

How much should I charge for handmade cards? At least your break-even price after fees, and usually enough to keep about 30%. In the example that is $4.32 for a batched card and $9.26 for a 15-minute one-off.

How much should I charge for handmade Christmas cards? Price them the same way, but batch the designs and sell packs. In the example a pack of 10 needs $41.17 for a 30% margin.

Is "3 for $10" a good deal for me? Only if your cost is low. At $2.76 a card it keeps $1.31 for three cards.

Should I count my time if card making is a hobby? If you sell, yes: otherwise the price never covers the hours, and you cannot tell which designs are worth repeating.

Selling at a fair? Check the stall pays first: is a craft fair worth it. New to pricing? Start with the general formula: how to price handmade items.
