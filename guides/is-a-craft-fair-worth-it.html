Is a craft fair worth it? Booth fee, profit per hour and a slow-day test

A craft fair is worth it when what is left after the booth fee, application fee, travel, card fees and the cost of the stock you sell is more than zero on a cautious sales figure, and when that profit, divided by every hour the fair takes, pays you at least the hourly rate you want. Busy fairs with high booth fees often earn more in total but less per hour, and they lose more on a rainy day. This guide shows how to work it out before you apply, with two example fairs side by side.

1. List every cost of the event

The booth fee is the cost everyone sees. The others add up quickly:

  • application or jury fee, even if you are not accepted next time;
  • travel there and back, every day of the event, at a cost per km or mile that covers fuel and wear;
  • parking, a hotel for multi-day events, and food you would not otherwise buy;
  • a share of your display: a tent, tables, lights and signs wear out, so spread their price over the events you expect them to last;
  • card fees: usually a percentage of each card sale plus a small fixed amount per payment.

event costs = booth + application + travel + parking + food + display share

2. Count only the stock you sell

Unsold stock goes home with you and can be sold later, so it is not a cost of the fair. What the fair costs you is the materials and packaging of the items that sell. If you know your cost of goods as a share of your prices (for example, items that sell at $20 cost $6 in materials and packaging: 30%), use that share.

profit = sales − cost of goods sold − card fees − event costs

In this guide your making time is not in the cost of goods: the profit is what pays you for the fair and for making the stock.

3. Count every hour, not just selling hours

A six-hour fair is rarely six hours of your time. Add loading the car, travel, setup, teardown and unpacking. Then divide:

profit per hour = profit ÷ all hours the fair takes

Compare the result with the hourly rate you want for your work. If you have not set one, our guide on working out your hourly rate shows how.

4. Find the sales you need to break even

Each dollar of sales leaves you a little less than a dollar after the stock and card fees. Divide the event costs by that amount to see how much you must sell before the fair pays for itself:

break-even sales = event costs ÷ (1 − cost of goods % − card fees as a share of sales)

Then compare break-even with your cautious sales figure. A fair whose break-even sits close to what you expect to sell is a gamble on the weather and the crowd.

Card fees work out at about 2.24 cents per dollar of sales here, so each dollar of sales leaves $0.6776 towards the event costs. That is where the break-even figures come from: $575 ÷ 0.6776 and $104 ÷ 0.6776.

The city fair earns almost twice as much in total, but the village fair pays slightly more per hour, and it needs to sell less than a quarter of its expected sales to cover its costs. The city fair needs nearly half.

5. Test a slow day before you apply

Now halve the sales, as a rainy day or a poorly promoted event can. The city fair takes $900 and keeps $34.84: about $1.70 an hour for two long days. The village fair takes $325 and keeps $116.22, $11.62 an hour. High booth fees turn a slow weekend into almost nothing, while a cheap fair stays positive.

So the question is not only "is it worth it if it goes well?", but "can I live with the result if it goes badly?". If you have no past results for a fair, plan with a cautious figure, and treat the first year as a test.

6. Count what is worth something but is not a sale

Some value does not show up in the till: buyers who take your card and order later, a shop owner who asks about wholesale, or a newsletter sign-up. If a fair repeatedly brings these, note them next to the profit. Be honest with yourself, though: a fair that never pays for itself on sales needs very clear later orders to justify it.

7. Rank your fairs after each season

After each event, write down the real sales, costs and hours. At the end of the season, sort your fairs by profit per hour and by profit on a slow day. Re-apply to the top of the list, and drop or replace the bottom.
