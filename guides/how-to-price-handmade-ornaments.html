How much should I charge for handmade ornaments?

To price handmade ornaments, add up what one ornament really costs you (materials, the box, your minutes at an hourly rate and a share of running costs), then price so you keep a margin after selling fees. In our worked example a painted wooden ornament costs $6.45 to make. It breaks even at $7.40, keeps a 34.67% margin at $12.00, and loses $2.18 at $5.00, a price you will often see on sale. Charge extra for names, and check any "3 for" deal against a single sale.

The short answer: true cost, then fees, then margin

Ornaments are small, cheap to make and sold in large numbers, so pricing mistakes add up fast. Sellers have asked the same questions on maker forums for nearly twenty years: should the price follow the wood or the design, and am I too cheap? Three numbers answer them for your own ornaments:

  • True cost: materials, packaging, your time and a share of running costs, per ornament.
  • Break-even price: the price at which a sale pays that cost and the selling fees, and nothing more.
  • Your price: break-even plus the margin you want to keep.

Step 1: cost one ornament

Cost a batch, then divide. Count the blank or base, paint, glue and finish, the hook and ribbon, and the box or bag. Time a whole batch from start to packed, divide by the number of ornaments, and pay those minutes at your hourly rate (see how to work out your hourly rate). Add a small share of running costs: tools, workspace, software.

Materials are only $1.85 of the $6.45. One vendor guide puts materials for most handmade ornaments at $0.50 to $3.00 a piece (TheCraftMap, Apr 2026). The rest, mostly your time, is the part that a quick "materials times two" price leaves out.

Step 2: find your break-even price after fees

Most selling and payment fees are a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. The example uses an invented fee of 9.5% of the price plus $0.25 per order, the same example rate as our gift set guide; use your own.

break-even price = (true cost + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee %)
price for a target margin = (true cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − margin %)

Break-even: (6.45 + 0.25) ÷ 0.905 = $7.40. Check: fees on $7.40 are $0.70 + $0.25 = $0.95, and 7.40 − 6.45 − 0.95 = $0.00. For a 30% margin: (6.45 + 0.25) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.30) = $11.07, so $12.00 is a sensible shelf price.

Fees = price × 9.5%, rounded to the cent, + $0.25. Margin = price − fees − $6.45 true cost. Earned per making hour = (price − fees − $1.85 materials − $0.60 running costs) ÷ 0.2 hours: what each hour of making earns before you pay yourself.

At $5.00 each hour of making earns $9.10, against the $20.00 you set. At $12.00 it earns $40.80. Vendor guides put most handmade ornaments at $5 to $25 (TheCraftMap) or $8 to $25 (TheCraftMap, Jun 2026), so a price that pays you sits well inside the usual range.

Step 4: price the name or date

Personalised ornaments take longer, so cost the extra minutes. Adding a name takes 5 more minutes, $1.67 of your time, so the personalised ornament costs $8.12. At $12.00 + $4.00 = $16.00, fees are $1.77 and the ornament keeps $6.11 (38.19%), $1.95 more than the plain one at $12.00. TheCraftMap suggests charging $3 to $5 for a custom name, date or detail (another part of the same guide says $3 to $8); either way, check the extra price covers the extra time.

Personalised orders also have a last safe date before Christmas: see when to stop taking custom Christmas orders.

Step 5: check a "3 for" set against a single sale

A set saves you two boxes and two order fees, but gives up price. In the example the set ships in one box per set, so the set costs 3 × 6.00 + 0.45 = $18.45; three singles in one order still use three boxes.

At this cost, the popular "3 for $25" keeps $3.92, $0.24 less than one ornament sold alone at $12.00 ($4.16). A set at $30.00 (a $6.00 or 16.67% saving on three singles) keeps $8.45, $4.29 more than one single, and three singles in one order keep $12.98. "A set of three for $25" is a common suggestion (TheCraftMap); whether it suits you depends on your cost. Here, three ornaments in a $25.00 set earn less than one sold alone, because nearly all their margin went into the saving. Price a set so it keeps clearly more than a single sale. Rules of thumb you will see

A common vendor formula is (materials + labour + overhead) × 2.5 (TheCraftMap). For the example: 6.45 × 2.5 = $16.13. Rounded to $16.00, that ornament keeps $7.78 after fees, a 48.63% margin. That is a rule of thumb, not a rule: it ignores fees, so use it as a ceiling test next to the margin method above, not as the answer.

Plan the batch before November

The US National Retail Federation counts the winter holiday season as 1 November to 31 December, and reports that about two in five holiday shoppers start browsing and buying before November (NRF). TheCraftMap suggests 100 to 200 ornaments across 10 to 15 designs for a full-day fair. Price first, then plan the hours:

120 ornaments × 12 minutes = 24 hours. At 2 hours on each weekday evening that is 12 evenings: start Monday 2 November, finish Tuesday 17 November, 10 days before Black Friday (Friday 27 November 2026). Their true cost is $774.00; sold at $12.00 each they bring in $1,440.00 before fees.

Common questions

How much should I charge for handmade ornaments? At least your break-even price after fees, and usually enough to keep a margin of around 30% on top of paying your time. In the example that is $12.00 for a $6.45 ornament.

How much should I sell homemade ornaments for at a craft fair? The same method works; use your card reader's fee instead of an online fee, and add the booth fee as a running cost spread over the ornaments you expect to sell.

Should ornaments be priced by the material or by the design? By the true cost, which includes both: a dearer wood raises materials, and a detailed design raises minutes. Cost each design on its own line.

Am I pricing my ornaments too cheap? If your price is near break-even, or your earned-per-hour figure is below the rate you set, yes.

How much should I charge for a set of ornaments? Enough that the set keeps clearly more than one single sale. Work out the set's cost with one box, then test prices as in step 5.

Making ornaments with a laser, a 3D printer or clay? Cost the piece with how to price laser engraving (or, for cups, engraved tumblers), how to price 3D prints or how to price pottery.
