How to keep track of orders for a small craft business

To keep track of orders in a small craft business, keep one order log with a row for every order: the date it came in, what it is, the ship-by date, the making hours it needs and its status. Sort the log by ship-by date, and once a day add up the hours still booked for each ship-by date and compare them with the making hours you have left before it. In the worked example below, six holiday orders need 42.00 hours and the maker has 56.00 making hours before the last ship date, so every order fits with 14.00 hours to spare.

1. The five columns that matter

An order log does not need to be clever. It needs to be complete, so that nothing lives only in your inbox or your head. One row per order, with these five columns:

  • Date received. When the order came in. It settles who was first when two orders want the same days.
  • What. The items and quantities, plus any custom details (names, colours, sizes) or a short reference to where those details are written down.
  • Ship-by date. The day the parcel must leave you, not the day the customer wants it. Work it out from your carrier's last recommended date and the time it takes you to pack.
  • Making hours. Quantity × your timed hours per item. Time a few pieces with a timer rather than guessing, and include finishing and packing.
  • Status. One word: new, making, made, shipped. Add at risk when the daily check says an order will not be ready in time.

Put every order in the same log, wherever it came from: your online shop, a message, a craft fair commission or a friend. Most order mix-ups happen when orders sit in different places.

2. Sort by ship-by date, not by date received

Work on orders in ship-by order. A big order that came in last week but ships first goes to the top, even if a small one has been waiting longer. Date received stays in the log for fairness and for your records, but the ship-by date decides what you make today.

3. The daily hours check

Once a day, for each ship-by date in your log, add up the hours of every unfinished order due on or before that date. Then count the making hours you have before it: working days × your making hours per day. If the hours booked are less than the hours you have, those orders fit. If not, you know today which orders are at risk, while you can still tell the customer or ask for a later date.

spare hours = making hours before the ship-by date − hours booked for orders due by then

Check every ship-by date, not only the last one. A late order can look fine in the overall total while an earlier deadline is already over-booked.

5. When to say no

That same Monday, a customer asks for 5 more custom stockings, shipped by Thursday 17 December. That is 5 × 3.50 = 17.50 hours. Add it to the Thu 17 Dec line: 37.50 + 17.50 = 55.00 hours booked against 52.00 available, 3.00 hours short. The order does not fit as asked.

Four stockings (14.00 hours) would just fit: 51.50 hours booked by Thursday 17 December and 56.00 by Friday 18 December, leaving 0.00 hours on the Friday line for a slow day, a failed piece or a sick day. Three stockings (10.50 hours) leave 4.00 hours spare on the Thursday line and 3.50 on the Friday line. Offer three by that date, or all five with a later ship-by date. Saying so on Monday is far easier than explaining a late parcel on 23 December.

Your cut-off for new orders is the day the spare hours run out. For a full method, see when to stop taking custom Christmas orders.

6. Keep the log short and current

  • Update it the moment an order arrives, not at the end of the day. A log that is a day behind gives a wrong daily check.
  • Move shipped orders out of the way. Keep them in the log for your records, but filter or sort them to the bottom so the daily check only counts work still to do.
  • Re-time your items in the busy season. If ornaments take 0.90 hours instead of 0.75, the whole plan changes. Update the hours per item and run the check again.
  • Keep some hours unbooked. Packing, messages and supply runs grow in December. The example books every making hour only to show the arithmetic.

Ready-made stock uses the same hours as orders. If you also make stock for markets or your shop, plan it with how much holiday stock to make and subtract it before you take more orders. Open orders are one of the records worth keeping all year; see what to keep track of in a handmade business.

Common questions

What is the simplest way to keep track of orders?

One list with one row per order and the five columns above, kept in one place. A paper notebook works for a few orders a week; a spreadsheet makes the sorting and the daily sum faster. Inventory software company Craftybase, writing about its own users, says "Manual tracking (spreadsheets, notebooks, memory) typically starts breaking down around 3-7 orders per day", and that "About 50% of currently-active makers are getting fewer than 1 order per day on average" (Craftybase, 6 April 2026). That is one vendor's data, not a rule.

How do I keep track of custom orders?

The same way, with the custom details in the What column or a reference to where they are written down. Time custom pieces separately: they often take longer than stock items. For pricing them, see how to price custom orders.

Can online and craft fair orders go in one log?

Yes, and they should, because they use the same making hours. Add a column for where the order came from if you want to compare channels later.

How much busier is the holiday season?

It depends on your shop; last year's log is the most useful guide. As a rough vendor figure, Craftybase says "Q4 (October–December) typically drives 2-4x the daily volume of slower months" in its users' data. Start the log before the rush so the habit is in place when it arrives.
