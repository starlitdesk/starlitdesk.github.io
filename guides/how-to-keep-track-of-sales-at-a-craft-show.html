How to keep track of sales at a craft show

To keep track of sales at a craft show, count every item you pack on a sheet with one line per product, tick the line each time that product sells, scan your stock every 30 minutes or so, count what comes home, and then match the ticks to the money: card reader total plus cash counted. In the worked example below, the ticks add up to $692.00 but the money says $710.00; the $18.00 gap is one pair of earrings sold but never ticked, and one more pair is simply missing.

1. Before the show: one line per product, counted

A tally sheet only works if it starts from a real count. Craftybase, which makes inventory software for makers, puts it this way: "Before you leave for the fair, fill in the product names and opening quantities for everything you're bringing." (Craftybase). Give each product its own line with its price, and count it as you pack it, not from memory. Write down the cash float you start with too, so you can work out cash sales at the end.

Pricing the table as well? How to display prices at a craft fair shows prices with one sign per price group, each rounded up to an easy cash amount.

2. During the show: tick every sale on its product's line

The same Craftybase template gives the rule: "Every time you make a sale, tick or cross off the line." Ticks by product matter more than one daily total. Made Urban, a craft show organiser, says: "You must track exactly which products you sold and how many of each." (Made Urban). That is what tells you what to make more of and what to pack less of next time.

Keep the sheet and a pen where you take payment, so ticking is part of every sale. When the table is busy, the shopper comes first; the same article advises: "Don’t let tracking numbers distract you from selling."

3. Every 30 minutes or so: a quick stock scan

Busy moments are when ticks get missed. Craftybase suggests: "Do a quick scan of your stock every 30 minutes or so and reconcile." Compare what is on the table with what the sheet says should be there. A missed tick found ten minutes later is easy to add; one found that evening is a guess.

4. After the show: count what came home

Unpack and count each product before it goes back on the shelf. For each line:

gap = packed − ticked as sold − came home

A gap of zero means the line balances. A gap means an untracked sale, a breakage, a sample given away or an item that went missing.

5. Match the ticks to the money

Next, add up what you actually took:

money taken = card reader total + (cash at the end − cash float)

Then value the ticks at their prices. If money taken is higher than the ticked sales, some sales were not ticked; if it is lower, check for discounts, refunds or a miscount of cash. If your card reader app can show sales by item, compare its item counts with your card sales too. Together, the stock gap and the money gap tell you which missing items were sold and which were lost.

The same evening, add the fair to your weekly log: one row for card sales and one for cash sales, with the card fee from the card reader app, as in how to keep track of sales and expenses. Common questions

What is the easiest way to track sales at a craft show? A paper tally sheet with one line per product and its packed count, a tick per sale, and a count of what comes home. It needs no power or signal.

Do I need to track cash and card sales separately? Yes. The card reader keeps its own total; cash leaves no record unless you count your float at the start and your cash at the end.

What if the numbers do not match? Check discounts, refunds and the cash count first, then compare the money gap with the stock gap by product, as in the example. A small gap you can explain is normal; a gap you cannot explain at every fair is worth watching.
