How to Raise Your Prices (and How Many Sales You Can Lose)

Raise your prices when your costs have gone up or you have not reviewed them in a year. Before you pick a number, work out what you keep per sale at the new price: that tells you how many sales you can lose and still earn the same. In our example a 10% rise on a $24 item can lose 4 of 40 monthly sales and still come out ahead.

Signs it is time to raise

One software vendor's blog for makers lists the usual signs, including "Your material costs have gone up." and "You haven't reviewed your prices in more than 12 months." It suggests a review "ideally at the start of each calendar year or financial year", and also when a key material rises "by more than 10%" (Craftybase, 10 Apr 2026). If your price no longer covers your cost and a fair wage, start with the floor price in how to price handmade items.

What you keep per sale, now and after the rise

Look at what each sale leaves you after the payment fee and materials. Your time is paid from this. All inputs in the example are invented: a $24.00 item, $6.50 of materials, a payment fee of 3% + $0.30 and 40 sales a month.

kept per sale = price − fee − materials
sales needed = kept per month now ÷ kept per sale at the new price
drop you can absorb = 1 − sales needed ÷ sales now

Now: the fee on $24.00 is $1.02, so each sale keeps $16.48, and 40 sales keep $659.20 a month.

At $26.40, 36 sales keep 36 × $18.81 = $677.16, above today's $659.20, while 35 sales keep $658.35, just below it. So you can lose 4 of 40 sales and still come out ahead. At $28.00 you can lose 7: 33 × $20.36 = $671.88, while 32 sales keep $651.52.

When your materials went up too

Say materials rise by $1.00, to $7.50. At the old $24.00 price each sale now keeps $15.48, so 40 sales keep $619.20: $40.00 a month less. To get back to $659.20 you would need 42.58, so 43 sales.

How much to raise: small steps or one jump

Advice differs. A vendor guide for small businesses prefers "small, regular increases of 3-5% annually", and says to "specify both percentage and pound amount changes" when you tell customers (Xero UK guide, 15 May 2026). The same guide says "a 10% price increase can maintain profitability even if volumes decline by 14%", citing the accounting body ICAEW; we have not read the ICAEW original. That holds only when what you keep is about 60% of the price: 10 ÷ (60 + 10) = 14.29%. Our example keeps more of each sale (16.48 ÷ 24.00 = 68.67%), so its 10% rise absorbs less: 12.38%.

A craft business site's rule of thumb is "Start with a 10–15% bump." and "Increase prices on bestsellers first." (Craft Professional). The maker vendor blog above warns against raising too little: "Don't raise by $0.50 when you need to raise by $6." Whichever you choose, run your numbers through the table first.

When to raise: January and other natural points

One small-business software vendor says "the rule of thumb is to raise prices at least once a year", and suggests announcing "near year's end to take effect January 1st", in November "before the busy holiday season" (Sage). New Year's Day 2027 is a Friday. Other natural points are a new product range or a new season's materials order.

How to tell customers

Keep the notice short. The maker vendor blog's advice is "Short. Honest. No grovelling, no over-explaining.", and it advises against a pre-emptive discount, which "signals the new price isn't 'real'". Give the new price, the date and, if you like, one reason. For example:

From Friday 1 January 2027 our 8 oz candles will be $26.40 (up from $24.00). Material costs have risen, and this keeps the quality the same.

Tell regular and wholesale customers first, and give them time to order at the old price if you wish.

Common questions

When should I raise my prices? When your costs rise or you have not reviewed prices for a year. Many makers do it from the start of the year.

How much should I raise prices by? Enough that your new kept per sale covers your costs and pay. In the example a 10% rise to $26.40 keeps $18.81 a sale instead of $16.48.

Will I lose customers if I raise prices? Some may go, and you cannot know how many in advance. Makers in one forum thread report keeping most customers, for example "for 98% of my customers they keep coming back" (Square Community thread, 23 May 2024), but that is an anecdote, not data. The table tells you how many you can afford to lose.

Should I discount after a price rise? Be careful: a discount gives back part of the rise. See Black Friday discount maths for the same kept-per-sale check in the other direction.
