How to keep a maintenance log for a rental property

To keep a maintenance log for a rental, write one row per job: date, unit (or "shared"), what was done, type (routine, repair or replacement), who did it, cost and where the invoice is. Split shared jobs across units, total each unit for the year and compare it with the rent. In our example four units cost $3,075.00 in a year, 6.29% of $48,900.00 rent, and one water heater makes up $1,380.00 of it.

1. What to write on each row

A maintenance log is a list of jobs that were done, one row each. A Quebec landlord association recommends "recording the date and nature of the work, before-and-after photos, the service provider involved," plus the costs, with the invoices attached (CORPIQ, 24 Aug 2026). For each job, write:

  • the date the problem was reported and the date the job was done;
  • the property and the unit, or "shared" for a roof, gutter or hallway job;
  • what was wrong and what was done, in a few words;
  • the type: routine, repair or replacement (section 2);
  • who did it and what it cost;
  • the date you paid and where the invoice and photos are filed.

Photos do not need a column of their own: a file name or folder in the notes is enough. The same association suggests you "review the log annually or quarterly to ensure it remains up to date."

2. Routine, repair or replacement

Give every job one of three types and keep to them:

  • Routine: planned upkeep that comes round again, such as a heating tune-up, filters, gutters or alarm batteries.
  • Repair: fixing something that broke, such as a leaking tap or a dishwasher pump.
  • Replacement: swapping a whole item at the end of its life, such as a water heater.

The split matters when you look back. Routine costs are roughly the same each year, repairs move around, and one replacement can make a single year look expensive. Keep them in the same log, with the type on each row, so you can total the year with and without replacements. What counts as a repair for tax depends on your country; this guide does not cover it.

3. Shared jobs: split them per unit

Some jobs serve the whole building. Log them once, as "shared", then split the cost evenly across the units so each unit's yearly total is complete. In the example, gutter cleaning on 2 buildings costs 2 × $180.00 = $360.00 and alarm batteries cost 8 × $6.50 = $52.00, so shared jobs come to $412.00, or $412.00 ÷ 4 = $103.00 per unit. An even split is the simplest rule; if your units differ a lot in size, you can split by floor area instead, as long as you use the same rule every year.

5. Check rules of thumb against your own log

Some landlords budget for upkeep with a rule of thumb. Two that you will often see, both from property businesses rather than official sources:

  • "Maintenance and repairs cost about 10% of the rent yearly." Another: "Set aside $1 per square foot for annual maintenance costs." (Nestwell help page, a property software company, updated 13 Oct 2023)
  • "One general rule is to save 1% of the property’s value per year." (Evernest, a property manager, undated)

That is the point of the log: after a year or two you can see what your own units cost instead of guessing. How much you set aside is your decision; this guide only shows the arithmetic.

6. Recurring jobs and next-due dates

A checklist says what is due; the log says what was done. Put the routine jobs on a short list with the date last done and the date next due:

  • Smoke alarms: "Test the alarm monthly." and "Replace the batteries at least once every year." for alarms with 9-volt batteries; "Smoke alarms do not last forever. Get new smoke alarms every 10 years." (U.S. Fire Administration)
  • Carbon monoxide alarms: "CPSC recommends consumers replace the batteries in their smoke and CO alarms annually and test the alarms monthly." (CPSC)
  • Filters and heating: "Inspect, clean, or change air filters once a month in your central air conditioner, furnace, and/or heat pump." ENERGY STAR also suggests you "check the cooling system in the spring and the heating system in the fall." (ENERGY STAR)
  • Other yearly jobs some landlords schedule, from the property manager above: "flush your water heaters at least once a year" and "cleaning your rental property’s gutters every year."

7. Spot repeat repairs

Sort the log by unit and look for the same item twice. In the example, B-1 had two plumbing jobs on the same sink: $85.00 + $120.00 = $205.00 in one year. Two or three small repairs on one item are a reason to look at the cause, or to weigh a replacement, before the next call-out. The landlord association above says a log is "particularly valuable when a problem reoccurs," and that "if the issue is recurring, it may be time to resolve it permanently".

Common questions

What should a rental property maintenance log include? One row per job: dates reported, done and paid, property and unit, what was done, type, who did it, cost, and where the invoice and photos are filed.

Is a maintenance checklist the same as a log? No. A checklist is what is due; a log is what was done and what it cost. Use both: the checklist tells you when, the log tells you how much.

How much do landlords budget for maintenance? Some use rules of thumb such as 10% of rent, $1 per square foot or 1% of the property value. In the example those give $3,000.00 to $5,200.00 for the same units, so check any rule against your own log after a year.

Should repairs and replacements go in the same log? Yes, in the same log with a separate type, so you can total the year with and without replacements.
