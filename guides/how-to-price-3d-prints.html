How to price 3D prints: filament, machine time and failed prints, not just grams

Pricing 3D prints by weight is quick: grams times a rate, done. It is also how many print shops end up busy and barely profitable, because a part's cost is not only filament. Printer time, power, worn nozzles, failed prints, setup and finishing all count. This guide shows a method that adds them up for any part, with a worked example.

1. Filament: cost per gram from the spool

Take the spool price and the net filament weight on the label:

cost per gram = spool price ÷ grams on the spool

Then use the part weight your slicer reports, including supports, brim and purge. Supports you snap off and throw away were still bought.

2. Machine time: a cost per print hour

Your printer wears out, uses power and eats parts. Turn that into an hourly rate:

  • Depreciation: printer price ÷ the print hours you expect it to last (your own estimate).
  • Power: average draw in kW × your electricity price per kWh. Average draw is far below the power supply's rating once the bed is heated; a plug-in meter tells you yours.
  • Maintenance: nozzles, build plates, belts and so on, spread per hour.

Add the three, then multiply by the print hours per plate from your slicer. If a plate holds several parts, divide by the parts per plate.

3. Failed prints are a cost, not bad luck

Every shop has failures: warping, spaghetti, layer shifts, a print knocked off the bed. If one plate in ten fails, each good part has to carry the filament and machine time of the failed ones:

cost per good part = (filament + machine time) ÷ (1 − failure rate)

Use your real failure rate from the last few weeks. New materials and tall, thin parts usually fail more. If a failure also costs you setup time, add that too.

4. Your time, packaging and overhead

Printing runs on its own, but slicing, starting a plate, removing supports, sanding and packing do not. Time setup per plate and finishing per part, and pay yourself an hourly rate you would accept. Add packaging and a small allowance for overhead such as tools, glue, software and spare parts.

5. Price so your margin survives the fees

Selling fees are usually a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. To keep the margin you want after fees:

price = (cost per part + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

With an example fee of 9.5% plus $0.45 per order and a 30% target margin: (13.60 + 0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.30) = $23.23. Rounded up to $24, the organiser keeps $7.67 after fees and costs, a 32% margin, while paying you $18 an hour for the 25 minutes of hands-on work.

Now the per-gram shortcut: at $0.10 a gram, 120 g comes to $12.00. After $1.59 in fees and $13.60 in costs, that sale loses $3.19. Even counting your time as free, it leaves only $4.31 for 25 minutes of work. The filament was the smallest part of the problem.

6. Small parts: fill the plate, mind the order fee

For small parts, printing many to a plate changes everything. In the example's set-up, 20 keychains of 8 g each on a 5-hour plate with a 5% failure rate cost about $0.25 each in filament, machine time and failures, plus about $0.75 of labour (setup spread over 20, plus 2 minutes of finishing each). At that size the fixed fee per order matters more than the plastic, so small items usually sell better in sets or as add-ons than one at a time.

7. Check each product and re-check often

Run the same calculation for every product and sort by margin: big, slow, support-heavy parts are often the ones losing money. Re-check when filament prices move, when you change material, and when your failure rate changes. In a spreadsheet, that is a few minutes.
