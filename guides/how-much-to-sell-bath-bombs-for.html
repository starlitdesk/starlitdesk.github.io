How much to sell bath bombs for

Add up one bath bomb's share of the batch, its wrap, your time and a little overhead, then price so your margin is left after selling fees. In our example a bomb costs $4.14 to make and needs about $7.59; three times the materials ($5.43) would keep only 32 cents after fees, and a gift box of three needs $24.00. Bath bombs look cheap to make because the powders are cheap; this guide adds up the costs that are easy to miss, with a worked example.

1. Cost the batch, then divide by the bombs that survive

You buy baking soda, citric acid, oils and fragrance by the bag or bottle and use part of each in a batch. Price every line the batch uses, add them up, and divide by the bombs you can actually sell. A bomb that cracks coming out of the mould was still paid for, so the good ones carry its cost:

materials per bomb = batch cost ÷ bombs that survive

One soap-making supplier prices a basic batch from its own list: "32 oz baking soda - $5.98", "16 oz citric acid - 6.99", "2 oz oil - $0.75", "0.6 oz Polysorbate 80 - $1.79" and "1 oz fragrance oil - $3.30", and says "This recipe makes 10-12 3.8 ounce bath bombs." (supplier's bath bomb cost article, read Sat 17 Oct 2026). Those are one supplier's list prices, not market rates; use what you pay, including shipping.

Illustrative figures only; every input in this guide's example is invented apart from the five quoted ingredient prices. Use your own.

2. Wrap and label per bomb

The same supplier notes that "Bath bombs need a very dry environment" and that "If you plan to sell or give away your bath bombs, you may want to wrap them in plastic." (supplier's storage article). Shrink wrap, a label and the minutes to apply them are a cost on every bomb you sell.

3. Count your time

Mixing, moulding, unmoulding, drying and cleaning up take time for the whole batch; wrapping takes time per bomb. Time a batch, divide by the bombs that survive, then add the wrapping minutes. A software vendor's guide to selling bath bombs puts it plainly: "The most common mistake: underpricing because you've forgotten to include your labour." (vendor's bath bomb business guide, published 16 Feb 2021). Pay yourself an hourly rate you would accept; how to work out your hourly rate shows how to set one.

4. Price so your margin survives the fees

Selling fees are usually a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. To keep the margin you want after fees:

price floor = (cost per bomb + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

6. Why "3x materials" falls short

The same vendor guide gives "3.5-5x your material cost is a common starting point" as a rule of thumb, and says "retail prices of $6-$12 are realistic for standard sizes" at craft markets. A markup only works on the full cost, though. Three times the materials is 3 × $1.81 = $5.43: fees $0.97, cost $4.14, and 5.43 − 0.97 − 4.14 = $0.32, 5.89% of the price. At 3.5x ($6.34) the bomb keeps $1.15. Your time is paid inside the cost, but the business keeps very little until the price passes the floor.

The supplier's own sums stop at materials: "these homemade bath bombs cost $2.48 each, or on the high side, $2.98", against "around $5.00 for a 4 oz bath bomb" at a big chain store. Their total counts whole bottles of the extras, so it runs higher than the materials line here. Neither figure includes wrap, time or fees, which is why a price matched to a chain store can lose money for a maker.

8. Wholesale: check the rule of thumb against your full cost

The vendor guide's wholesale rule is "2-2.5x your material cost (assuming retail is at least 4x)". In the example, 2.5 × $1.81 = $4.53 a bomb with no selling fee keeps 4.53 − 4.14 = $0.39; 2 × $1.81 = $3.62 is below the $4.14 cost. Test any wholesale rule against your full cost first; wholesale vs retail price shows how to set both.

9. December timing

For 100 bombs the example needs 10 batches (110 moulded, with one cracking in each): 10 × 45 minutes = 7.50 hours, plus 100 × 1.5 minutes of wrapping = 2.50 hours, 10.00 hours in all. For shipped orders in the contiguous US, the US Postal Service recommends sending by "Dec. 17" with its Ground Advantage service, which is Thursday 17 December 2026 (2026 holiday dates). Common questions

How much should I sell my bath bombs for? At least your floor: cost per bomb plus the fixed fee, divided by what is left after the fee % and your target margin. In the example that is $7.59, rounded up to $8.00.

How much can you sell bath bombs for? One vendor guide calls $6 to $12 realistic for standard sizes at craft markets. That is one vendor's opinion, not market data; check it against your own floor first.

How much do bath bombs sell for? One supplier quotes around $5.00 for a 4 oz bath bomb at a big chain store. A maker who wraps by hand and pays fees usually needs more than that: in the example, $5.43 keeps 32 cents.

How much to sell homemade bath bombs for? Start from the full cost, not from other sellers' prices. If your floor sits above the going rate, look at your costs, your bomb size or a gift box.

Do I need a license to sell bath bombs? Rules depend on where you sell; check with your local authority.
