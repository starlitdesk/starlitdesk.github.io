How much to charge for a pottery class

To price a pottery class, add each student's clay, glaze and firing, then split your teaching, prep and finishing time and the studio's running costs over the seats you expect to fill, and price for your margin after booking fees. In the example, a 2-hour wheel taster costs $24.80 a seat when all 8 seats sell but $44.80 with 4, so a $65.00 seat keeps $38.01 in a full class and $18.01 in a half-full one.

1. Per-student costs: clay, glaze, firing, consumables

Some costs grow with every student who books. Count them per seat:

  • Clay: the weight one student uses, including what goes back in the reclaim bucket, at your bag price per pound or kilo.
  • Glaze: what one student's finished piece takes.
  • Firing: the student's share of the bisque and glaze firings. Our pottery pricing guide shows how to cost a kiln load and split it by shelf space.
  • Consumables: sponges, wire tools, wrapping for the finished piece, anything a student uses up.

For a sense of scale, a lessons directory says "A typical 25-pound bag of clay costs around $25" and that firing, when studios charge it separately, runs "$5 to $20 per piece" (lessons directory, read Sun 18 Oct 2026). Use your own supplier prices.

2. Per-class costs: teaching, setup, finishing and the studio

Other costs are the same whether 2 or 8 students turn up:

  • Teaching hours: the class itself.
  • Setup and clean-up: wedging clay, setting out wheels and tools, cleaning afterwards.
  • Finishing students' pieces: trimming, glazing or loading the kiln after the class, if you do it for them. This is easy to forget, and it is real time.
  • Studio share: a slice of rent, power, wheel wear and insurance for the hours the class uses the room.

Pay your hours at a rate you would accept; our guide on working out your hourly rate shows one way to set it.

3. Split the class costs over the seats you fill

Divide the per-class costs by the seats you expect to sell, not by the seats you have. That is the step that changes the price most: the same class costs much more per seat when it runs half full.

cost per seat = per-student costs + per-class costs ÷ seats sold

4. Price for fees and your margin

Booking and card fees are usually a percentage of the price plus a fixed amount per booking. To cover the cost after fees, and to keep a margin on top:

break-even = (cost per seat + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee %)
price for a margin = (cost per seat + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − margin %)

5. Worked example: a 2-hour wheel taster for 8

Invented figures, labelled illustrative: a 2-hour wheel class with 8 wheels, your time at $30.00 an hour, and a booking fee of an assumed 2.9% + $0.30 per seat, each seat booked and paid separately. Use your own costs and your own fee.

Per student: clay 2.5 lb at $25.00 for 25 lb = $2.50, glaze $0.60, the share of two firings $1.20 and consumables $0.50, so $4.80 a seat. Per class: 4 hours (2 teaching + 1 setup and clean-up + 1 trimming and glazing students' pieces) × $30.00 = $120.00, plus a $40.00 studio share, so $160.00 a class. 6. Minimum seats to run a class

Each $65.00 seat leaves 65.00 − 2.19 − 4.80 = $58.01 toward the $160.00 class cost. 160.00 ÷ 58.01 = 2.76, so 3 seats cover it (3 × 58.01 = $174.03, $14.03 over) and 2 do not (2 × 58.01 = $116.02, $43.98 short). Many studios set a minimum: one UK listing for a 2-hour taster says "Public dates for this class require at least 2 guests to run" (class-booking listing, Peterborough, read Sun 18 Oct 2026). Work out your own from your costs, and say in the booking terms what happens if a date does not fill.

7. What the class price includes

Say exactly what a seat buys, because firing and glazing are where expectations differ. One arts district's 2-hour date-night wheel workshop at $85 per person says "Registration includes all materials, aprons, clay, tools, firing, glazing, and one finished piece for each participant", ready "in approximately three weeks" (event page, event Sat 18 Jul 2026). The UK taster above, at £55 a guest, includes "The clay, tuition, the first firing" and charges a small extra fee to come back and glaze. Either works, as long as the cost of every firing you promise is in the per-student line, and the pick-up wait is in the description.

Common questions

How much does a pottery class cost? The sources we read put a single class at about $35 to $150. A lessons directory lists "$50 – $150 per class", wheel classes "$50 – $150" and hand-building "$40 – $120"; a business-plan vendor's blog lists a single session at "$35–$99" and a workshop or special event at "$55–$99" (vendor blog, 22 Oct 2025). These are examples, not market rates; your own cost per seat is the floor.

Should firing and glazing be included in the class price? Include what you promise. If the seat includes a glazed, fired piece, the glaze and both firings belong in the per-student cost, and finishing time belongs in the class costs.

How many students do I need to run a class? Divide the class costs by what each seat leaves after fees and materials and round up. In the example that is 3 seats at $65.00.

Should I charge less per seat for groups? Some studios do: the UK taster drops from £55 to £50 a guest for 6 or more. A group discount works when it fills seats that would stay empty; check the price for each seat count in the table above before you offer one.

How much should I charge for multi-week courses? Use the same sum per session, then multiply. The lessons directory lists "$200 – $450+ (6 to 8 weeks)" for a course.

Pricing other bookings over fixed time? How much to charge for Christmas mini sessions shares a day's fixed costs over the families who book, the same way. Selling the pots too? See how to price pottery for wholesale.
