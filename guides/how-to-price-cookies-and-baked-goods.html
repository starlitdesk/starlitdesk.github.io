How to price cookies and baked goods: cost per cookie and per box, with your time and fees

Ask a home baker what a box of cookies costs to make and you will usually hear the price of butter and chocolate. The real cost also includes the flour you barely notice, the oven, the box, the cookies that break, and the hours you spend mixing, baking and packing. This guide shows a simple way to cost a batch, turn it into a cost per cookie and per box, and set a price that still leaves you something after fees.

1. Turn every pack price into a cost per gram

You buy ingredients in packs and use them in grams. So start by working out what one gram (or one millilitre, or one egg) of each ingredient costs:

cost per gram = pack price ÷ pack size in grams

A 2.27 kg (5 lb) bag of flour at $4.50 costs about $0.002 a gram. That looks like nothing, until you multiply it by 560 g of flour in a batch. Do this once for every ingredient and keep the list: when a price changes, you update one number.

2. Cost the whole batch, then divide by what you can sell

Multiply each ingredient's cost per gram by the amount in your recipe and add them up. Small items such as vanilla, salt and raising agents can share one small allowance. Then divide by the cookies you can actually sell, not the number that came out of the oven. If two of 24 cookies break or get eaten as tasters, you are selling 22, and those 22 carry the cost of all 24.

3. Do not forget the oven

Oven energy is small per cookie but real. A rough estimate: oven power (kW) × share of time the element is on × hours of baking × your electricity price. An oven element switches on and off to hold its temperature, so it does not draw full power the whole time; 50% is a guess you can refine from your meter or bill.

4. Add packaging, your time and overhead

Price boxes, bags, labels and tissue from the pack you bought. Then time a batch from weighing to packing, choose an hourly rate you would accept, and add a small amount per box for overhead: a share of things like trays, mixer wear, piping bags and market pitch fees. For most home bakers, labour is the biggest cost in a box of cookies, which is why it is so tempting to leave it out.

5. Price so your margin survives the fees

Whether you sell online or take cards at a market, most payment and selling fees are a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. To keep the margin you want after fees:

price = (cost per box + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

With an example fee of 3% plus $0.30 per order and a 30% target margin: (23.94 + 0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.03 − 0.30) = $36.18. Rounded up to $37, the box keeps $11.65 after fees and costs (a 31% margin) and pays you $18 an hour.

If that looks high, look at where it comes from: over 60% of the cost is your time. Ingredients alone are $6.81, and a box priced at an "ingredients × 2" shortcut ($13.62) would leave you $3.70 for about 49 minutes of work, roughly $4.50 an hour, once the box, fees and overhead are paid.

6. Change the batch, not just the price

Labour per cookie falls fast when a batch gets bigger, because weighing, cleaning up and setting out trays take about the same time either way. In the example, a double batch (48 cookies, 3 lost, 70 minutes of baking, 2 hours of work) costs $18.64 per box of 12 instead of $23.94. At 30% margin the same formula gives $28.27, so $29 keeps $9.19 a box. Time your own double batch before you rely on this: ovens, mixers and cooling space set the limit.

7. Price boxes and singles separately

A fixed fee per order matters much more on a single cookie than on a box, and singles need their own bag. Cost each format on its own line rather than dividing the box price by 12. The same goes for decorated cookies: icing time can double the labour, so they deserve their own batch costing.

8. Re-check when prices move

Butter, chocolate and eggs change price often. Re-run your numbers when a key ingredient changes, when you change your box, and before the holiday rush. Kept in a spreadsheet, that takes minutes.
