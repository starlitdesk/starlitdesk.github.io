How to price laser engraving: machine cost per minute, pieces per sheet and setup time

To price laser engraving, cost each piece from its share of the sheet, laser minutes at your machine's cost per minute and other parts, spread setup time over the batch, add handling time and overhead, then price for your margin after fees: a plywood coaster from a batch of 12 costs $2.57 and sells for $5.00 keeping 30%, but a single personalised coaster costs $8.07 and the formula asks $14.08. Laser shops often price by machine minutes, or by "material plus a bit". Both can work, but they hide two costs: what the machine itself costs you per minute, and the setup time for a job (design, alignment, test burn, cleaning) that a big batch spreads thin and a single personalised piece carries alone. This guide shows how to cost one piece from a machine rate, pieces per sheet and setup spread over the batch, and price it so your margin survives the fees.

1. Work out your machine rate

Spread the machine's price over the hours you expect it to last, then add electricity (the laser plus air assist and extraction) and consumables such as lenses and filters. Expected life and average power draw are your own estimates; your meter and your maintenance log are the best sources.

machine rate per hour = machine price ÷ expected life hours + kW × price per kWh + consumables per hour
machine rate per minute = rate per hour ÷ 60

2. Count pieces per sheet

For items cut from sheet stock, divide the sheet by the item size plus the gap you leave between pieces, across and down, and round each down to whole pieces. This simple count does not rotate items, so a clever layout can fit more; it is a safe starting point.

pieces per sheet = ⌊width ÷ (item width + gap)⌋ × ⌊height ÷ (item height + gap)⌋
material per piece = sheet price ÷ pieces per sheet

Engraving ready-made blanks instead? Use the blank's price as the material cost.

3. Cost per piece

cost per piece = material per piece + laser minutes × machine rate per minute + other (finish, findings, packaging)

4. Spread setup over the batch, then price for fees

Setup takes about the same time for one piece or twenty. Divide it by the batch size and add the handling time per piece (loading, weeding, finishing, packing). Pay yourself for those minutes at an hourly rate you would accept, add a small overhead allowance per piece, and price for your margin after fees:

labour minutes per piece = setup minutes ÷ batch size + handling minutes
price = (cost per piece + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

Worked example: coasters, keychains and slate

Illustrative figures only: a $2,400 machine expected to last 5,000 hours, 0.5 kW average draw, electricity at $0.17 per kWh, $0.30 per hour of consumables, labour at $18 an hour and $0.50 overhead per piece. Use your own machine, materials and prices.

The machine rate is $0.48 + $0.085 + $0.30 = $0.865 an hour, or about $0.0144 a minute. A 300 × 400 mm sheet of birch plywood fits 3 × 4 = 12 coasters of 95 × 95 mm with 5 mm gaps, and 6 × 10 = 60 acrylic keychains of 50 × 30 mm with 3 mm gaps.

One personalised coaster is a different product

The $5.00 coaster assumes 20 minutes of setup shared by a batch of 12. A personalised coaster with its own name needs its own setup: 22 labour minutes, and a full cost of $8.07. Sold at $5.00, it loses $3.99. The formula suggests $14.08 for a single personalised coaster; at $15.00 it keeps $5.06 (34%).

A personalised set of four shares one setup and one order fee. Four coasters cost $14.28 in total, the formula suggests $24.35 for the set, and at $25.00 it keeps $7.90 (32%), about $6.25 a coaster. Selling personalised pieces as sets, or with a clear single-piece price, protects the margin that batch pricing hides.

5. Re-check when something changes

Re-run the numbers when sheet or blank prices change, when your electricity tariff changes, when a lens or tube replacement shows your consumables estimate was off, and when you time real setups. Jobs with long setups and small batches are where a price review pays off first.
