What one yes is worth: getting handmade products into stores

Pick shops that already sell work like yours, email the buyer with a one-page line sheet, follow up once or twice, and send samples only when asked. Then do the sums: a round of pitching costs time and samples, and in our illustrative example 20 emails bring 2 opening orders that leave you $19.40 short of what the round cost. The first reorder turns it into $56.80 ahead. The reorder is where the money is.

1. Pick shops that fit, close to home first

Look for shops whose shelves already hold work at your price and in your style: gift shops, boutiques, galleries, museum shops and cafes with a retail corner. Shops you can visit as a customer are the easiest start, because you can see what sells, how it is displayed and what it costs. A UK craft magazine's guide, quoting shop owners, puts it plainly: "There's nothing wrong with starting small and local and expanding from there." (Gathered, updated 8 Aug 2024).

Write the list down before you email anyone: shop, buyer's name if you can find it, date emailed, reply, sample sent, follow-up date. That list is the record you will use to judge the round later.

2. What to send: an email and a one-page line sheet

"Email should be your first point of contact." (a product-business blog, 26 Jun). Shop owners in the magazine guide agree: "Many retailers, although not all, prefer to receive submissions by email." Keep the email short: who you are, why your work fits their shop, and the line sheet attached.

A line sheet is one page of facts a buyer needs to order. Made Urban's blog for makers lists what a line sheet should include: business name and logo, product photos, product names, descriptions and item numbers, wholesale price and suggested retail price, minimum order quantities, payment terms, lead times and shipping info, and contact details (Made Urban, 27 Oct 2025). You do not need a designer: you can "start with a simple PDF". The same guide says "retailers expect to buy your products at 50% of their retail price", so set your wholesale price first with our guide to wholesale vs retail pricing.

Minimums are your choice. Craftybase, which makes inventory software for makers, gives one starting point: "A common starting point for handmade makers is a $150–$200 minimum order value, with a 6-unit minimum per product." It adds: "That’s not a rule, just a range that tends to work for small-batch production." (Craftybase, 5 Jun 2026). Its sample payment line reads "Most small makers require 50% upfront, balance on shipping", which is one practice, not a standard.

3. Follow up once or twice, then stop

The product-business blog suggests a calendar reminder "for a week or two later to follow up". Then move on: the shop owners in the magazine guide say you will not hear back from everyone, and not everyone is your customer. Our rule of thumb, not an industry standard: one follow-up after about two weeks, a second only if the shop's next buying round is coming up, then mark the shop as closed on your list.

Timing matters for seasonal work. Made Urban notes that larger retailers "may be buying Christmas products for their stores in August", and Craftybase's example line sheet says "Place orders by September 15 for holiday delivery." For products that sell all year, Made Urban suggests that once a shop stocks you, "you can touch base after a month or two to see how your product is selling".

4. Samples: only when asked, and count them as a cost

Do not arrive with a bag of samples. One shop owner in the magazine guide says: "Turning up out of the blue with a bag of samples definitely puts us on the spot." When a buyer asks for a sample, send one, and assume it is gone: the product-business blog notes that shops usually do not return sample products. A sample costs what it cost you to make plus postage, and it belongs in the cost of the round.

If a shop is unsure, the same blog suggests offering your products on consignment "for a specific period of time" as a way in. The record-keeping for that is in our guide to keeping track of consignment stock.

Worked example: 20 shops, 2 orders, 1 reorder

Illustrative figures only; every input is invented, including the replies and orders: no source we found gives reply or order rates. Hand-printed tea towels sell at $24.00 retail, $12.00 wholesale (50%), and cost $5.40 to make, so you keep $6.60 a towel at wholesale. An opening order is 3 designs × 6 towels = 18 towels; a reorder is 2 designs × 6 = 12 towels. Packing costs $3.00 per order. Pitching takes 0.5 hours per shop, valued at $20.00 an hour. Opening orders minus the round = orders × 115.80 − 251.00. Cost per shop won = 251.00 ÷ orders.

With a single order, that shop needs two reorders before the round pays: −$135.20 + 2 × $76.20 = $17.20. Three levers move the numbers: a better-fitting list (more replies from the same hours), a larger opening order, and shops that reorder. Of the three, reorders cost you no more pitching, so track which shops sell through your stock and come back.

Common questions

How do I find stockists for my product? Start with shops near you that already sell work like yours at a similar price, and list them with the buyer's name, the date you emailed and the reply.

How do I sell handmade items to stores? Email the buyer a short note with a one-page line sheet (photos, wholesale and suggested retail prices, minimums, terms, lead times, contact), follow up once or twice, and send a sample when asked.

How do I sell handmade jewelry to boutiques? The same way: pick boutiques that already carry jewelry at your price, send a line sheet by email, and check that your wholesale price is half your retail price and still covers your cost.

Should I send free samples to shops? Send them when a buyer asks, and expect not to get them back. Count each sample, with postage, as part of the cost of finding shops: in the example, 5 samples are $51.00 of a $251.00 round.
