Is my craft business making a profit? A monthly check by channel, with profit per hour

To see whether your craft business is making a profit, do one check a month: add up what each sales channel brought in, subtract the fees, the postage you paid, the materials you used and your running costs, and what is left is your profit before your own pay. Then divide it by the hours you worked. In the worked example below, a maker sells $1,237.00 in October and keeps $679.91 before pay, which looks healthy, but over 52 hours that is $13.08 an hour, well short of the $20 an hour the maker wants.

1. The monthly profit formula

A profit and loss statement "shows a business' revenues, expenses, and profit or losses over a specific period" (FDIC Money Smart for Small Business). For a small handmade business, one month on one page is enough:

profit before your pay = sales − fees − postage − materials used − running costs
profit per hour = profit before your pay ÷ hours worked

Made Urban, which runs craft markets, puts it plainly: "You should be checking in each month to see how your business is profiting" (Made Urban).

2. Step 1: sales by channel

List what each place you sell brought in: your online shop, each fair or market, wholesale orders, commissions. For online sales, include the shipping the buyer paid, because that money came in too and its cost comes off in step 2. Keep the channels apart.

3. Step 2: fees and postage

Take fees from your payout statements, not from a rate you remember: listing fees, transaction and payment fees, and card reader fees at fairs. On many marketplaces, fees apply to the shipping you charge as well as the item, which is one more reason to use the statement. Then add the postage you actually paid for the month's orders.

4. Step 3: materials used, not materials bought

This is the step most monthly checks get wrong. If you stock up on wax, yarn or clay in October, the whole bill is not a cost of October's sales. Count what you have on the shelf at the start and at the end of the month and use the standard cost-of-goods formula:

materials used = opening stock + materials bought − closing stock

Value the stock at what you paid for it. A rough count is fine.

5. Step 4: running costs

Add the costs that keep the business going: packaging, booth and application fees, software and subscriptions, ads, studio costs, tools spread over the months you use them. Made Urban groups them as production, studio space, marketing online, marketing offline, sales and administration, which is a useful checklist so nothing is missed.

6. Profit per hour: the number that tells you if prices work

A 55% margin sounds good, and on its own it would say "keep going". The hourly figure says something else: $679.91 pays $20 an hour for about 34 of the 52 hours worked. Made Urban goes one step further and counts your wage as a cost before profit; in that view this month made a loss of $360.09. 8. If the month is negative, or the hourly figure is low

  • Prices first. If profit per hour is under your target month after month, the price does not pay for your time. Re-price with the formula in how to price handmade items.
  • Then costs. Look at the biggest lines: a fee you could avoid, postage you undercharge (see how much to charge for shipping), or an ad that brings no orders.
  • Then channels. Drop or change the channel that leaves least per hour.
  • Look at three months, not one. A stock-up month or a single big fair can swing one month either way.

"Sell more" alone rarely fixes a low hourly figure: more sales at the same price need more hours too.

Common questions

How long does it take to make a profit? There is no standard answer; it depends on your prices, costs and hours. The monthly check tells you where you are: once profit before your pay stays above zero for a few months in a row, you know the business covers its costs, and the hourly figure tells you how far it is from paying you.

What is the difference between gross and net profit? Gross profit is sales minus the cost of goods sold (here, materials used). Net profit is what remains after all costs, expenses and taxes (FDIC). This guide's "profit before your pay" is a simple monthly figure before tax; it does not deal with tax.

If I wanted to improve sales, where would I start? With the channel table: put more time into the channel that leaves most per hour, and fix the price or costs of the one that leaves least, before you add a new channel.
