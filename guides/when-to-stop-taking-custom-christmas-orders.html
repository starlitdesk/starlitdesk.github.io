When to stop taking custom Christmas orders: work back from the last shipping date

Stop taking custom Christmas orders on the day your booked work fills the hours you have left before your last shipping date, not on the shipping date itself. To find that day, work back from the carrier's last recommended send date, take off packing days and a small buffer, count the hours you can really make things, and subtract the orders you have already accepted. For many small makers the answer comes weeks earlier than they expect. This guide shows the method step by step, with a worked example.

1. Start from the carrier's last send date

Every carrier publishes a last recommended date to send each service for delivery before Christmas. For 2026, USPS lists Thursday 17 December for Ground Advantage, Friday 18 December for Priority Mail and Saturday 19 December for Priority Mail Express, for addresses in the contiguous US. International and military dates are earlier, and other carriers and national postal services publish their own. These are recommendations, not guarantees, and carriers can change them, so check the carrier's page again before you promise a date.

Shipping from Canada? Canada Post's holiday sending guidelines (page dated October 2026) list, between major urban centres: Regular Parcel by 10 December nationally (16 December regional, 21 December local), Expedited Parcel by 14 December nationally and Xpresspost by 22 December nationally. To the US: Tracked Packet by 11 December, Expedited Parcel USA by 14 December and Xpresspost USA by 17 December.

Use the service most of your customers choose. If you offer a faster service at a higher price, it buys you a day or two, but only for the customers who pay for it.

2. Take off packing days and a buffer

An order is not ready to ship the moment it is finished. Allow the days you need to pack, label and drop off parcels, then add a buffer for the things that always happen in December: a supply delay, a cold, a batch that has to be remade.

make-by date = last send date − packing days − buffer days

Count in your own working days. If you do not work weekends, a "two-day buffer" before a Monday starts on the Thursday before.

3. Count the hours you really have

List your working days from today to the make-by date, remove days off, and multiply by the hours a day you can actually spend making. Be honest here: in the busy season, messages, packing and admin eat into making time, so 4 hours of real production in a 7-hour day is common.

hours available = working days × production hours per day

4. Subtract what you have already booked

Add up the making time of every order you have already accepted and every piece of stock you plan to make for the season. Time a few pieces with a timer rather than guessing; custom work usually takes longer than you remember.

free hours = hours available − booked hours
orders you can still take = free hours ÷ hours per custom order

Your cut-off is the day the free hours run out. If new requests arrive at a steady pace, you can estimate that day in advance and announce it.

Worked example: a maker shipping by Ground Advantage

Each custom order takes about 3 hours, so 28 free hours hold 9 more custom orders (27 hours), with 1 hour to spare. If about 2 custom requests arrive each working day, those 9 places are gone by Friday 20 November. That is the last day to say yes, almost four weeks before the shipping date.

Now the common habit of taking orders "until early December". Accepting 2 a day until Friday 4 December adds 14 working days × 6 hours = 84 hours. With the 52 already booked, that is 136 hours of work against 80 available: 56 hours, or 14 working days, more than the season holds. Some orders would ship late, or you would work every evening and weekend to catch up.

Offering Priority Mail (Friday 18 December) as a paid upgrade adds one working day, 4 hours, which is one more custom order for those customers only.

5. Close custom orders, keep selling ready-made stock

A cut-off for custom work does not have to stop sales. After the cut-off, you can keep selling pieces you have already made, or offer a short list of simple personalisations that take minutes, not hours. Say clearly on each listing and in your shop announcement which kind of order still arrives before Christmas.

6. Tell customers early, and in their words

Post your cut-off date as soon as you know it: "Custom orders for Christmas close Friday 20 November. Ready-made pieces ship until Thursday 17 December." Put it where buyers look before they order, and repeat it in your order confirmation. A clear date reduces the late messages asking for a miracle.

7. Re-check every week

Booked hours change every day: orders come in, a piece takes longer than planned, you lose a day to illness. Re-run the numbers once a week, and if your free hours run out early, move the cut-off forward rather than squeezing in one more order.
