How to price crochet and knitted items: find what you really earn per hour

Crochet and knitting take longer than most buyers imagine. A beanie can be three or four hours of work; a blanket can be forty. That is why pricing yarn pieces feels impossible: the "fair" price often looks far higher than what people pay. This guide shows a way to price that starts from facts, then helps you decide what to do when the numbers do not fit the market.

1. Cost your yarn by weight

The most reliable way to cost yarn is to weigh the finished piece. From the skein label, take the price and the weight per skein, and work out the cost per gram (or per ounce):

yarn cost per gram = skein price ÷ grams per skein

Multiply by the weight of the finished item. If you work from yardage instead, the same idea works with yards or metres. Weighing beats counting skeins, because a piece that uses 2.2 skeins should not be costed as 3, and the part-skein you have left over is worth something.

2. Add the other materials

Buttons, safety eyes, lining, zips, labels, tags and packaging all count. Price each from the pack you bought: a pack of 50 labels at $10 is $0.20 a label.

3. Time yourself honestly

Time one piece from start to finish, including weaving in ends, blocking and photographing. Use a timer for a few pieces; estimates tend to be low. If a pattern is new to you, time the second or third piece instead of the first.

4. Run the numbers in both directions

Most pricing guides only go one way: costs plus hourly rate gives a price. That is useful, but for yarn work it is just as useful to go the other way: start from the price you charge now and see what you actually earn per hour.

earn per hour = (price − fees − materials) ÷ hours

And to set a price that pays your rate and keeps a margin after fees:

price = (materials + hours × hourly rate + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

At the current price of $35, with an example fee of 9.5% plus $0.45 per order: $35 − $3.33 − $0.45 = $31.23 after fees. Minus materials, $17.03 is left for 3.5 hours: $4.86 an hour.

At $15 an hour with no margin, the price is (14.20 + 52.50 + 0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095) = $74.20. With a 15% margin on top, it is $88.94.

5. What to do with the gap

Seeing $4.86 an hour is uncomfortable, but it is useful. You now have four honest options, and you can choose per product:

  • Raise the price towards the calculated one, and describe the yarn, fibre and time so buyers see why. Some buyers want heirloom pieces and will pay for them.
  • Lower the time: chunkier yarn, simpler stitch patterns, or smaller items make the same skill earn more per hour.
  • Lower material cost without lowering quality: buy yarn in bulk for best sellers, or choose a different fibre for a cheaper line.
  • Price consciously below your rate for pieces you make for the joy of it, knowing the real number. That is a choice, not an accident.

Sort all your products by earn-per-hour. Items at the bottom of the list are where a small change helps most.

6. Patterns and selling rights

If you sell pieces made from someone else's pattern, read that pattern's terms for selling finished items. Some designers allow it freely, some ask for credit, and some do not allow it.

7. Keep it current

Re-check when yarn prices or fees change, and whenever you notice an item taking longer than you thought. A spreadsheet with your yarns and items saved makes this quick.
