How much holiday stock to make: a capacity-first plan with a demand estimate and buffer

To work out how much holiday stock to make, start from capacity, not hope: multiply the hours you can make things each week by the weeks left and by the units you finish per hour, then compare that with a holiday demand estimate (your normal month times a seasonal multiplier) plus a 10-20% safety buffer. If the plan fits inside your capacity, make it; if not, cut the range or the target before you buy materials. In the worked example below, a candle maker with 12 hours a week can make 216 candles by late November and plans 148, with 17 hours to spare.

1. Count your capacity first

Most stock plans start with a sales goal and work forward. The trouble is that the goal ignores the one thing you cannot buy more of in November: your own hours. Start with them instead:

capacity = making hours per week × weeks left × units per hour

This is the same formula one inventory software company for makers, Craftybase, recommends: plan inventory before revenue. Two details make it honest:

  • Not every hour is a making hour. Packing, photos, messages, markets and admin grow in the busy season. Keep a share of your hours for them; the example keeps 25%.
  • Units per hour comes from a timer, not memory. Time a full batch from setup to clean-up and divide by the pieces you got out of it.

2. Pick a finish date, then count back

Holiday buying starts earlier than many makers plan for. The US National Retail Federation reports that about two in five holiday shoppers start browsing and buying before November, and that November and December together have averaged about 19% of total US retail sales over the last five years (NRF Winter Holiday FAQs). Those are figures for all retail, not handmade sellers, but the direction is clear: stock needs to be ready before the rush, not during it.

Choose a date to finish most of your holiday making, such as the weekend before Black Friday, and count the weeks from today. If a product needs curing or drying time, the finish date for making it is earlier still: cold-process soap, for example, usually needs a full six-week cure, so soap for December has to be made by about late October at the latest (Bramble Berry).

3. Estimate holiday demand from your normal month

Take the units you sell in a normal month and multiply by a seasonal factor for November and December. Your own sales from last year are the best factor. If you have none, a starting point: across more than 1,200 of its sellers, Craftybase reports November at about 50% above an average month and December at about 70% above (multipliers of 1.5 and 1.7), with Black Friday week often 2.5 to 3 times a normal week. That is one vendor's data, and its method is not published, so treat it as a rough guide and replace it with your own numbers as soon as you have them.

holiday demand = normal month × November factor + normal month × December factor

4. Add a safety buffer

Some pieces crack, some batches fail and some products sell faster than expected. Add a buffer on top of demand and round up. Craftybase suggests 10-15% when ordering materials and 15-20% for holiday material plans; the example uses 15%.

plan = holiday demand × (1 + buffer), rounded up

5. Compare the plan with capacity

If the plan is at or below capacity, you have room and can decide whether to use the spare hours for extra stock, custom orders or rest. If the plan is above capacity, something has to give, and it is better to decide now than in the second week of December.

Now the same maker with a busier shop: 80 candles in a normal month. Demand becomes 80 × 1.5 + 80 × 1.7 = 256 candles, and with the 15% buffer 294.4, rounded up to 295. Capacity is still 216, so the plan is 79 candles short. At 4 an hour, that is 19.75 extra making hours, about 3.3 more making hours every week for six weeks, on top of the 12 hours already planned.

6. When demand is bigger than capacity

You have three honest levers, and you can combine them:

  • Narrow the range. Make more of your best sellers and fewer slow or fiddly products. Faster products raise units per hour without longer days.
  • Lower the target. Plan to sell out early, and say clearly in your shop when holiday stock has gone, rather than promising pieces you cannot make.
  • Start earlier next year. Every week you add at the start of the window adds a full week of capacity. Note this year's real numbers so next year's plan starts from data.

Taking custom orders as well? Booked custom work uses the same hours, so subtract it before you plan stock. See when to stop taking custom Christmas orders. Selling at holiday markets? Split part of your plan into fair stock with how much inventory to bring to a craft fair.

7. Re-check every week

Once a week, compare what you have made and sold with the plan. If you are behind, use the levers above now; if a product is selling faster than its multiplier, move making hours to it. A plan that changes with your real numbers beats a perfect plan made once.
