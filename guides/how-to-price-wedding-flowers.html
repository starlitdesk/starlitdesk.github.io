How to price wedding flowers so the quote pays for every hour

To price wedding flowers, cost each design from its recipe (stems plus hardgoods), add a waste allowance on the flowers, multiply by the quantities, then add every hour the wedding takes, not only design time: processing, the consultation and proposal, delivery, setup and breakdown. Price that full cost for your fees and target margin, and check it against a wholesale multiplier. In the illustrative example below, a 23-hour wedding costs $1,308.33 to make and is quoted at $1,975.00.

1. Start from the order list: designs × quantities

A wedding is not one arrangement; it is an order list. Write down every design the couple wants and how many of each: bouquets, buttonholes, centerpieces, ceremony pieces. Each design gets one recipe, and the recipe is costed once, then multiplied by its quantity. That keeps a ten-table wedding from turning into ten separate guesses.

If you already price single designs this way, the method is the same as in how to price flower arrangements; a wedding just adds quantities and a lot more hours.

2. Cost each recipe, then add waste to the flowers only

For each recipe, add up the stems at your cost per stem and the hardgoods (ribbon, pins, foam, containers) at cost per unit. Then add a waste allowance to the flowers, because some stems will be damaged, short or not open in time. Hardgoods do not wilt, so they get no waste allowance.

How much waste? One floristry software vendor suggests a 15-20% buffer on wholesale (Curate), and a flower delivery company says florists order 10-20% more flowers than needed (Poppy). Your own records are the better number; the example uses 15%.

3. Count every hour, not just design time

Design time is the part you see. A wedding also takes processing (unpacking, cleaning and conditioning stems), the consultation and the written proposal, and delivery, setup and breakdown on the day. Poppy's breakdown puts bridal bouquets at 1-2 hours each, centerpieces at 30-60 minutes each, processing at 10-40 hours and delivery, setup and breakdown at 3-6 hours (Poppy). Curate notes that proposals alone take 2-4 hours each (Curate), and a cut-flower farm blog says a "small" wedding can take 15 to 25 hours of skilled labour (Beancount). These are vendor figures, not rules; time your own work.

Put a rate on every hour. If you have not set one, the hourly rate guide shows how.

5. Check it against a 3.5x markup (and see what it pays per hour)

Many florists price from wholesale cost with a multiplier. Curate describes a common formula as multiplying by 3.5 to 4.5 and adding a labour or design fee of 30 to 50 percent on top (Curate); a florist writing on her own costing gives 3 to 4 times wholesale as a rule of thumb (The Flower Scout), and florists in an older forum thread used anything from 2.5x to 5x on flowers (Florist 2.0 Community). There is no single markup.

6. The forgotten hours

Suppose the quote counted only design and delivery: 12 + 5 = 17 hours, labour $510.00, cost $1,128.33. The 30% floor would then be ($1,128.33 + $0.60) ÷ 0.67 = $1,684.97. But the wedding still takes 23 hours and costs $1,308.33. At $1,684.97 the fee is $51.15, profit is $1,684.97 − $51.15 − $1,308.33 = $325.49 (19.32%), and pay per real hour falls to ($325.49 + $690.00) ÷ 23 = $44.15.

Six unpriced hours of processing and consultation cost $290.03 of the quote ($1,975.00 − $1,684.97).

7. Quoting months ahead: a flower price buffer

A quote sent in October for a June wedding fixes the price eight months before you buy the stems. If flower prices rise 10% by June, the flowers including waste cost ($408.20 + $61.23) × 0.10 = $46.94 more. At the fixed quote of $1,975.00, profit falls from $606.82 to $559.88 (28.35%).

Two common answers: price a buffer into the quote (Beancount mentions a 10% contingency on the floral total; Beancount), or include a substitution clause that lets you swap stems of similar value. What your quote allows is between you and the couple: check your agreement.

8. What the booking retainer covers

Arithmetic only. Materials are $573.33 ÷ $1,975.00 = 29.03% of the quote. In the example, a 30% retainer of $592.50 covers the materials with $19.17 to spare, and the balance of $1,382.50 is due on Saturday 29 May 2027, 14 days before the wedding ($592.50 + $1,382.50 = $1,975.00). Common questions

How do I charge for wedding flowers? Add up the full cost (recipes × quantities, waste on the flowers, every hour, travel), then price it for your fees and margin. Use a multiplier as a check, not as the price.

How do I price a wedding bouquet? Cost it as one recipe. In the example the bridal bouquet is $48.00 of flowers + $7.20 waste + $4.50 hardgoods = $59.70 of materials, plus 70 minutes of design time at your hourly rate.

"How much would you charge for this?" It is a common question in florist forums (Curate). Nobody else knows your stem costs or your hours; cost the recipe yourself.

Is there a standard pricing formula? No. Florists use multipliers from 2.5x to 5x and treat labour differently. Use one as a check against your cost-based floor.
