How to calculate your handmade business profit for the year

Your profit for the year is your 12 months of sales, minus selling fees and postage, minus your running costs, minus the materials you actually used. Materials used is not what you bought: it is the stock you started the year with, plus what you bought, minus what is left when you count your shelves on 31 December. Divide the profit by the hours you worked to see what the year paid you per hour. In the illustrative example below, a candle maker sells $7,140.00 in the year and keeps $2,794.60 before pay: $5.37 an hour over 520 hours.

1. The yearly formula

A profit and loss statement is "A financial statement that shows a business' revenues, expenses, and profit or losses over a specific period." (FDIC Money Smart for Small Business). The year uses the same formula as our monthly profit check, with 12 months added up and one stock count at each end:

profit for the year = sales − fees and postage − materials used − running costs
profit per hour = profit for the year ÷ hours worked

A payments company's business guide suggests a small business may run a profit and loss statement "perhaps monthly", and that most businesses also do "an annual profit and loss statement to assist with their year-end financial reporting" (business resource article, 31 May 2024). The steps for each line are in the monthly check; this guide covers what changes when the period is a whole year.

2. Step 1: add up the 12 months

Total three lines from your monthly figures or your log: sales (including shipping the buyer paid), fees and postage you paid, and running costs (packaging, booth fees, software, ads, tools). If you keep a weekly log, as in how to keep track of sales and expenses, the year is 12 monthly totals of it. Do not add up the materials you bought yet: that line needs the stock count first.

3. Step 2: count your stock on 31 December

On the last day of the year, count what is on your shelves: raw materials and finished pieces not yet sold, wherever they are. Craftybase, which makes inventory software for makers, says to count finished goods in every location, including anything on consignment and photography samples, and to weigh materials rather than guess: "Don’t estimate." (Craftybase stocktake guide, 24 Mar 2026).

Value everything at cost: "Inventory is valued at cost — what you paid for the materials, not what you sell the finished goods for." A finished candle counts at the material cost that went into it, not at its price tag. Write the total down: it is this year's closing stock and next year's opening stock.

4. Step 3: materials used = opening stock + bought − closing stock

This is the standard cost-of-goods sum. AccountingCoach, an accounting education site, starts with "The cost of the retailer’s beginning inventory", adds the purchases, takes off "the cost of its ending inventory", and the result "Equals the cost of goods sold" (AccountingCoach). For a maker, inventory is your materials and unsold pieces:

materials used = stock on 1 January + materials bought in the year − stock on 31 December

Without the count, everything you bought looks used up, and stock still on the shelf is counted as a cost of this year's sales.

5. When the count and your records disagree

If you track stock as you go, the count will rarely match it exactly. In the example, the records say $820.00 of stock but the shelves hold $780.00: a gap of $40.00, or 4.88% of the recorded stock. Use the count, not the records, in the sum above. Craftybase's advice: "For small discrepancies, adjust your records to match reality and note the reason." Damaged or unsellable stock is written down, not kept at full cost. It also notes that "A quarterly count lets you catch discrepancies early".

6. How much of the year came in November and December

In the example, November and December bring in $3,130.00: 43.84% of the year's sales, against an average of $401.00 a month from January to October. The National Retail Federation counts the holiday season as November and December ("NRF defines the holiday season as Nov. 1 through Dec. 31.") and forecasts US retail sales in November and December to "grow 4.2% over 2025" (NRF, 15 Oct 2026). That is all US retail, not handmade, so treat it as background. Your own share is the number that matters: if two months carry close to half the year, the stock you make for them and the dates you stop taking orders matter as much as your prices. 7. Profit per hour, and what one price change does to it

A 39.14% margin looks healthy; $5.37 an hour does not. The hourly figure is the one that tells you whether your prices pay for your time. Compare it with your target from working out your hourly rate.

Common questions

How do I calculate profit for the year? Add up 12 months of sales, fees and postage, and running costs; work out materials used from your stock count (opening stock + bought − closing stock); then take fees, materials used and running costs off sales.

How do I work out my profit margin for the year? Divide the profit for the year by the year's sales. In the example, $2,794.60 ÷ $7,140.00 = 39.14%.

Do I count stock at cost or at selling price? At cost: what you paid for the materials, including those inside finished pieces, not their price tags.

What if my stock count doesn't match my records? Use the count, adjust the records and note the reason, as in the $40.00 example above.

Is yearly profit the same as taxable profit? Not necessarily. This guide is records and arithmetic only; check the rules where you live.
