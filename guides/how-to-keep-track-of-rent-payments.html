How to keep track of rent payments as a small landlord

To keep track of rent payments, write one row per unit per month: the month the rent is for, the rent due, the amount that arrived and the date it arrived. Compare each row with the due date plus your grace days to mark it Paid, Paid late, Part paid or Unpaid, and carry a running balance per unit. In our example four units owe $4,075.00 for September, $2,750.00 arrives (67.48% collected), and two tenants still owe a combined $1,525.00 at the end of the month.

1. One row per unit per month

A bank statement shows money in by date. A rent ledger shows rent by the month it is for. Keep those apart: rent for September that arrives on 3 October still belongs on the September row, with 3 October as the date received. For each row, write:

  • the month the rent is for and the unit (a short unit ID such as A-1);
  • the rent due for that month, from the lease;
  • the amount received and the date it arrived;
  • how it was paid and a short note (for example "agreed reduction" or "paid in two parts").

Make the row even when nothing arrives. An empty row with rent due is how an unpaid month shows up.

2. Decide when a payment counts as late

Take the due day from each lease. Many landlords also allow a few grace days before they treat rent as late; the number, and whether you may charge anything for late rent, depend on your lease and the rules where the property is. This guide does not cover those rules. For the ledger, one setting is enough:

late if received after: due date + grace days

With rent due on Tue 1 Sep and 5 grace days, a payment that arrives on or before Sun 6 Sep counts as on time. Use four words for each row and keep to them:

  • Paid: the full amount arrived by the cut-off.
  • Paid late: the full amount arrived, but after the cut-off.
  • Part paid: less than the full amount by the date you check, after the cut-off.
  • Unpaid: nothing arrived by the date you check, after the cut-off.

Check unit B-1: $950.00 due, $500.00 received on Thu 3 Sep, so 950.00 − 500.00 = $450.00 is still owed and the row is Part paid. Across the four units, $4,075.00 was due and $2,750.00 arrived: 4075.00 − 2750.00 = $1,325.00, so 67.48% of September's rent has arrived.

4. Carry a running balance per unit

A month on its own hides old debts. Add each month's "due minus received" to what the unit already owed. In the example, B-2 was $200.00 short in August, so its balance is not just September's rent:

balance to date = balance last month + rent due − rent received

B-2's balance to date is 200.00 + 875.00 − 0.00 = $1,075.00. B-1 owed nothing before September, so its balance is $450.00. Together the two units owe $450.00 + $1,075.00 = $1,525.00. A balance below zero means the tenant paid ahead; keep the row as it is rather than moving money between months.

5. Put expenses and repairs in the same file

Rent is only half of the picture. Write each payment you make for a property on its own row: date, property, category (insurance, repairs, utilities, fees, other), amount and where the receipt is filed. Log a repair once, with the date you paid it, so it is not counted twice. For September in the example:

6. A 10-minute monthly routine

  • After the cut-off, open the ledger and fill in every row for the month from your bank statement.
  • Look at every row that is not Paid, and check its balance to date.
  • Enter the month's expenses and any repairs paid.
  • Note leases that end in the next two months, so renewals do not surprise you.
  • If a tenant asks what they have paid, give them a statement for the months they ask about: rent due, received and balance.

Common questions

Should I record rent by the month it is for or the date it arrived? Both: the month it is for decides the row, and the date received decides whether it was late.

Is a part payment late? In this ledger a part payment after the cut-off is Part paid, and the rest stays in the balance until it arrives. What you may do about it depends on your lease and local rules.

What should I write down about tenants? As little as you need. A tenant reference such as T-01 is enough in the ledger; keep the file somewhere private.

Do I need property management software? For a handful of units a spreadsheet is enough if you fill it in every month. Software helps most when you want tenants to pay online or log repair requests themselves.
