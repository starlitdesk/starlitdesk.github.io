How much inventory to bring to a craft fair: sales target, sell-through and break-even

To decide how much inventory to bring to a craft fair, divide the sales you expect by your average price to get the number of items you will sell, then divide that by the share of your stock that usually sells (your sell-through). If you expect $800 in sales at an average of $17.65 an item and usually sell about 30% of what you bring, you need about 154 items (157 once each product is rounded up). This guide shows each step, how to split the total across your products, and how to check that the fair can pay for itself.

1. Set a realistic sales figure

Start from evidence, not hope. Your own results at the same fair last year are best. Next best: your results at similar fairs, or what the organiser tells you about footfall. For a first fair, choose a modest figure and treat the day as a test. Write the number down before you pack; it keeps the rest of the plan honest.

2. Turn sales into items

Work out your average price per item from your expected product mix, not from your most expensive piece. Then:

items you expect to sell = sales target ÷ average price per item

3. Divide by sell-through

You never sell everything you bring. Sell-through is the share of the items you pack that actually sell:

sell-through = items sold ÷ items packed
items to pack = items you expect to sell ÷ sell-through

Use your own past sell-through if you have it. If you do not, pick a cautious example figure for the first fair and measure the real one on the day. A full-looking table sells better than a sparse one, so it is normal to bring two to four times what you expect to sell.

Adjust for the kind of event. Some vendor guides use rough multipliers on your sales target, counted at retail value. These are rules of thumb from other sellers, not measured data: about 2× for a small local market, 2.5-3× for a busy fair you have sold at before, 3-4× for a holiday fair and 4× or more for a multi-day festival, leaning to the top of the range at your first event (TheCraftMap). The example's 30% sell-through works out to about 3.3× (1 ÷ 0.30), close to a holiday fair; for a small market, 50% sell-through gives 2×. Another rough guide is that about 2% of the shoppers who pass your table may buy (Made Urban), so ask the organiser for last year's visitor numbers. The sources do not agree on sell-through, so your own count after the fair beats any of them.

4. Split the total by product

Multiply the items to pack by each product's share of the items you sell, and round up. Give every product enough for a full display, even if you only expect to sell a few: a single print on a stand looks like a leftover.

5. Check the fair can pay for itself

Before you commit, work out how many items you must sell just to cover the booth and the trip:

event costs = booth fee + application fee + travel + other costs
break-even items = event costs ÷ (average price − average cost per item − card fee per item)

If the break-even number is close to what you expect to sell, the fair is a gamble. If it is a small share, the fair is low risk even on a slow day.

An $800 sales target at an average price of $17.65 means about 46 items sold (45.3, rounded up). At 30% sell-through that is 154 items to pack; rounding up each product gives 157 items.

Event costs come to $85 + $15 + $18 = $118. Card fees average $0.43 per item, so each item leaves $17.65 − $5.36 − $0.43 = $11.86 towards the costs. Break-even is 10 items, about a fifth of the 46 you expect to sell, so this fair is low risk.

If you do sell 46 items: $811.90 in sales, minus $246.56 cost of goods, $19.78 card fees (46 × $0.43) and $118 event costs, leaves $427.56. Over a 10-hour day of travel, setup and selling, that is $42.76 an hour, before the time you spent making the stock.

6. After the fair: measure your real sell-through

The example's 30% is a guess until you measure it. Count what you pack, log what you sell by item during the day, and count what comes home. The gap between packed, sold and returned also shows breakage, samples and missing items. After two or three fairs you will know your own sell-through per product, and you can pack less of what never sells and more of what sells out by noon.

7. Compare fairs by profit, not by sales

A busy fair with a high booth fee can earn less than a quiet one. Rank your events by profit after booth fees, card fees and travel, and by profit per hour, and use that ranking when you decide which fairs to apply for next season.

Before you pack, price each item first, so the average price and cost per item above are real. To decide whether a fair pays for your hours at all, see is a craft fair worth it?, and for a Christmas market, when to stop taking custom Christmas orders.
