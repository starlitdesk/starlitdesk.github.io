How to price custom orders for handmade items: extra time, deposits, revisions and rush fees

To price a custom order, start from the normal price of the item, add the extra time the custom work takes (messages, design, proof, changes and extra making) at your hourly rate, add any extra materials, and take a deposit before you start. A personalised $24 candle that takes 40 extra minutes at $20 an hour and a $0.60 custom label comes to $37.93; a flat 20% markup would charge $28.80 and undercharge by $9.13. A percentage markup is a quick check, not the price. This guide shows each step, plus deposits, revisions and rush fees.

1. The custom order formula

A custom piece is your normal product plus extra work. So price it the same way:

custom price = normal price + extra minutes × your hourly rate ÷ 60 + extra materials

The normal price should already cover your materials, making time, overhead, fees and margin; if you are not sure it does, start with how to price handmade items. Vendor guides describe the same building blocks: a base product cost, then a customisation fee, a rush fee if any, and shipping (TheCraftMap). Marmalead puts it plainly: the price of customising "has to obviously cover the costs of doing so", including the time the custom work takes (Marmalead).

2. Count the hidden minutes

Custom work takes time before you make anything. Time these for your next few custom orders, with a timer, not from memory:

  • messages back and forth to agree what the buyer wants;
  • design work: lettering, layout, choosing colours;
  • making and sending a proof, and waiting for the answer;
  • each round of changes;
  • extra making time: a slower setup, a one-off colour, a fiddly finish.

Pay those minutes at the same hourly rate you use for the rest of your pricing. Our hourly rate guide shows how to set one that also pays for admin time.

3. Flat markups: a check, not the price

Many guides suggest a flat percentage on top of the normal price. TheCraftMap gives a customisation markup of "typically 20-50%" by complexity: about 15-20% for a colour, scent or size change, 25-35% for personalisation such as a name or date, and 40-50% for a new design (TheCraftMap). These are rules of thumb from a vendor blog, not measured data. A percentage scales with the item price, while the extra work often does not: a 20-minute proof takes as long on a $12 item as on a $60 one. On low-priced items a percentage usually undercharges, as the example shows.

Even a 50% markup leaves the candle $1.93 short. The extra materials cost only $0.60: it is the 40 extra minutes that make a custom price. If your selling fees are a percentage of the sale, they also fall on the extra $13.93. To keep all of it, divide the extras by (1 − fee %): at an example fee of 10%, $13.93 ÷ 0.90 = $15.48, a custom price of $39.48.

4. Deposits and what is included

Take a deposit before you start design work, so a buyer who disappears after the proof has paid for your time. TheCraftMap suggests at least 50%, with the balance on pickup or delivery (TheCraftMap); treat that as a rule of thumb and check what your selling platform allows. In the example, 50% of $37.93 is $18.965, so the deposit is $18.97 (rounded half-up to the cent) and the balance is $18.96. To size the deposit against your materials and plan the later payments, see how much deposit to take on a custom order.

Say in writing what the price includes: in the example, one proof and one round of changes. Each extra round is priced the same way as the rest: 10 minutes at $20 an hour is $3.33. Buyers rarely mind a clear rule; they mind a surprise.

5. Rush orders

A rush order pushes other work back or takes your evenings. TheCraftMap suggests 25-50% extra for a fast turnaround, again as a rule of thumb (TheCraftMap). At 25%, the example's rush fee is $37.93 × 0.25 = $9.4825, so $9.48, and the rush price is $47.41. Only offer a rush you can keep, and remember that the shipping time does not get shorter: our guide on how much to charge for shipping covers the delivery side.

6. Quote a buffer, and set a holiday cut-off

Custom work runs late more often than stock work, because you wait for answers. TheCraftMap's advice: "If you think it'll take 2 weeks, quote 3" (TheCraftMap). Before Christmas, decide when to stop accepting custom work at all: our guide on when to stop taking custom Christmas orders works the date back from the last shipping day.

Common questions

How do I price my first custom order? Take the normal price of the closest product you sell, time the extra work as well as you can, and add it at your hourly rate with any extra materials, as in the example. After the order, compare your estimate with the real minutes and adjust the next quote.

How should I price custom pieces that are nothing like my usual products? Cost them from scratch, like a new product: materials, all your time, packaging, overhead and fees. Personalised laser and embroidery work is costed this way in our guides on laser engraving and machine embroidery.

What policies should I set for custom work? Write down the deposit, what the price includes (proofs and rounds of changes), the price of extra changes, the quoted turnaround and the rush fee. Check your platform's and your country's rules on refunds and cancellations for made-to-order items.
