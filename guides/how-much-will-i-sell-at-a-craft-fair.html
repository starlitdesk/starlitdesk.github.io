How much will I sell at a craft fair? A footfall forecast

Estimate craft fair sales as attendance × the share who stop at your table × the share of those who buy × your average sale. Work out a slow, middle and busy case, plan your budget on the slow one and pack for the busy one. The worked example below turns 3,000 visitors into a range of $226.80 to $1,270.08.

1. The forecast formula

Most of the people at a fair never reach your table, and most of those who do only look. So split the guess into two steps instead of one:

buyers = attendance × stop rate × buy rate
sales = buyers × average sale

The stop rate is the share of all visitors who stop at your table; the buy rate is the share of those who then buy. Made Urban suggests tracking exactly these two rates, with the example of 400 people stopping out of 1,000 visitors (40%) and 20 of those 400 buying (5%) (Made Urban, 7 stats to track). Those are illustrative numbers from one article, not a benchmark for every fair.

2. Getting each input

  • Attendance: ask the organiser for last year's visitor count, or look on the event's site and social media. Made Urban gives rough size tiers of 300+ shoppers for a small event, 2,000+ for a medium one and 10,000+ for a big one (Made Urban). These are rules of thumb.
  • Stop rate and buy rate: your own tally is the only good source. At your next fair, make a mark for every person who stops and every person who buys.
  • Last year's sales: if you sold at this fair before, divide those sales by your average sale to get last year's buyers, and use that as your middle case.

3. Average sale = average item price × items per sale

Many buyers take more than one item, so track how many items are in each sale as well as the price. Made Urban calls this units per transaction and suggests counting it at every fair. If your items average $18.00 and a sale holds 1.4 items on average, your average sale is $18.00 × 1.4 = $25.20. For a sense of scale, one vendor guide puts candle and soap sellers at a "$10-30 average sale" (TheCraftMap, vendor reports, not measured data).

5. Why one "2% buy" guess can mislead

A single conversion figure hides the two steps. Made Urban suggests guessing a conversion rate of 2% to 20% until you have your own stats, a range ten times wide. Apply a flat 2% to all 3,000 visitors and you get 60 buyers × $25.20 = $1,512.00, more than even the busy case above. Splitting the guess into stop rate and buy rate makes each number small enough to check against your own tally.

6. From forecast to stock: pack for the busy case

The forecast tells you how much you will sell; how much to bring depends on sell-through, the share of what you pack that sells. TheCraftMap says most vendors "sell between a quarter and a half of what they carry", and calls 40-60% a healthy range for most events (TheCraftMap, inventory management; rules of thumb).

In the example, the middle case sells 33.60 items. Divided by a 40% sell-through, the bottom of that healthy range, that is 33.60 ÷ 0.40 = 84 items, which also covers the busy case's 71. Split by each price tier's share of the items sold and rounded up per tier:

7. Price tiers: what running short and leftovers each cost

Every item a buyer wanted but you did not bring loses its margin: $12.60 on average here, $29.40 for a high-tier piece and $5.60 for a card. Every everyday item that goes home only ties up its cost, $5.40 on average, until the next fair. As TheCraftMap puts it, "unsold inventory is not spent money".

8. Seasonal stock after the last holiday fair

Christmas-only stock is different: it cannot wait a year as easily. Made Urban suggests finding a way to sell seasonal leftovers before the season ends, possibly with an incentive (Made Urban, after a craft fair). If you cleared an average item at 30% off (an invented figure), you would give up $18.00 × 30% = $5.40 of the full price. A missing sale costs $12.60, so here one missing sale costs 12.60 ÷ 5.40 = 2.33 times one cleared leftover. That is arithmetic, not a stocking rule: compare the two numbers for your own prices before you cut the pack.

9. After the fair: replace the guesses with your own counts

Count what you packed and what came home, tally stops and buyers during the day, and note the items in each sale. After one fair you have your own stop rate, buy rate, items per sale and sell-through; after two or three, your middle case is based on evidence instead of guesses. For holiday markets, set your custom Christmas order cut-off so making for the fair and custom orders do not collide.

Common questions

How much money can you make at a craft show? Work it out as a range, not one number: attendance × stop rate × buy rate × average sale, for a slow, middle and busy day. One vendor guide reports beginners grossing about $200-$800 per event and intermediate sellers $800-$2,500 (TheCraftMap); use that as a sanity check only, and remember it is sales, not profit.

What determines how much you will make? Four inputs: attendance, the share who stop, the share of those who buy, and your average sale. Then your costs decide how much of it you keep.

Why aren't my craft show sales higher? Find which input is low. Tally stops and buyers at your next fair: few stops points to your display or position, many stops but few buyers points to price, range or how you take payment.

Was a slow fair my table or the event? Low attendance is the event. A normal crowd with a low stop rate or buy rate is your table, and that you can change.
