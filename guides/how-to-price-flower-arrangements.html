How to price flower arrangements: cost per stem, waste, your time and where markup falls short

Many florists price with a markup: flowers times three, hardgoods times two and a half, plus a labour percentage. It is quick, and for flower-heavy designs it often leaves a healthy cushion. But a multiplier only sees what the materials cost, not how long the design takes, so a design that is mostly your time can come out underpriced. This guide shows how to cost a design from a cost per stem, add waste, hardgoods and your time, price it so your margin survives the fees, and then use a markup as a comparison rather than the only answer.

1. Turn bunch prices into a cost per stem

Flowers are bought by the bunch or the box, but designs use stems. Divide what you paid by the stems you got. Do the same for hardgoods bought in packs, such as vases, wrap, ribbon or water tubes, to get a cost per unit.

cost per stem = bunch price ÷ stems per bunch
cost per unit = pack price ÷ units per pack

2. Write a recipe for each design

A recipe lists the stems and hardgoods in one design. Multiply each line by its cost per stem or unit and add them up, keeping flowers and hardgoods apart, because only flowers wilt, snap or bruise.

3. Add waste to the flowers

Some stems never make it into a design: bent heads, bruised petals, stems that open too early. Add a waste percentage to the flower cost of each recipe. Your own records are the best source: count what you throw away over a few weeks.

flowers incl. waste = flower cost × (1 + waste %)
material cost = flowers incl. waste + hardgoods

4. Add your time, then price for fees

Time each design from conditioning the stems to wrapping it. Pay yourself for those minutes at an hourly rate you would accept, add a small overhead allowance, and price for your margin after fees:

full cost = material cost + labour minutes ÷ 60 × hourly rate + overhead
price = (full cost + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

5. Compare with a markup, and see where it falls short

Now the classic markup on the same designs: flowers incl. waste × 3, hardgoods × 2.5, plus 20% for labour. It gives $18.59 for the bud vase, $53.63 for the posy and $44.24 for the crown.

For the two flower-heavy designs, the markup price sits well above the cost-based price: at $53.63 the posy would keep $28.05 (52%). That cushion can pay for things this simple example leaves out, such as a cooler, a shop or unsold stock, so a markup is not wrong.

The crown is different. Most of its cost is an hour of your time, which the markup only sees as 20% of the flower and wire cost. At $44.24 it keeps $8.55 (19%), below the 30% target, while the cost-based price asks for $52.05. Designs that take a lot of wiring, gluing or binding, such as crowns, buttonholes and corsages, are where a markup quietly underprices your time.

A practical rule: the cost-based price is your floor. Use the markup price as a second opinion, and when it comes out lower than the floor, the design is labour-heavy and needs its own price.

6. Re-check when something changes

Flower prices move with the season and with holidays, so re-cost your best sellers when your supplier's prices change, when your waste records show a different rate, and when you time a design again. Wedding and event work quoted months ahead deserves a margin of safety for price changes, or a clause in your quote that allows for substitutions.
