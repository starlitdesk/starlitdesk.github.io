How to display prices at a craft fair

Put a price where every shopper can see it without asking: a tag on one-off pieces, and one sign per price group for everything else. Round each price up to an easy cash amount and check it still covers your costs and fees. In the example, 50 items in six product types need just four price signs ($5, $10, $15, $20), and rounding up keeps a 40.63% margin on the whole stall.

1. Why visible prices sell

Shoppers at a busy table rarely ask. A craft fair directory's blog puts it bluntly: "Nothing kills a sale faster than a customer having to ask "how much?" and then walking away embarrassed." The same article adds: "Clear pricing actually encourages browsing - people can self-select based on budget." (TheCraftMap, 5 Feb 2026). A crochet designer's list of display dos says the same: "Make sure you have tags on every item or signs listing your prices in large print." (crochet designer's blog, modified 22 Jul 2026).

2. Tags, category signs or a price board

TheCraftMap lists the options: "Individual price tags on every item", "Price signs for each category" and a menu-style board. Each suits a different kind of stock:

  • Tags for one-off pieces, where every item has its own price.
  • One sign per price group for products made in runs: a basket of keychains, a row of candles.
  • One price board for a short range. A label vendor's guide says: "If you sell only a handful of products – say, less than 10 – you may be better off displaying your prices on one big sign." (label vendor's guide, updated 23 Apr 2026).

Small cards in front of the goods work well on a table. A print vendor's blog suggests: "Set up table cards in front of products with the prices written on them." (print vendor's blog, 9 Oct 2026).

3. Count your signs: price groups, not items

You do not need a sign for every item, or even for every product. You need one for every price. If two products share a price and sit side by side, one sign covers both. Fewer prices means fewer signs, a tidier table and faster sums at the till.

4. Round to easy cash amounts, then check the maths

TheCraftMap's pricing article advises: "Price in round numbers or easy-to-make-change amounts: $5, $10, $15, $20, $25." and "Avoid prices like $17 or $23 that require complex change-making and slow down your line." It also says: "For products over $20, round numbers actually perform better at craft fairs." (TheCraftMap, 14 Feb 2026). These are one site's tips, not measured results.

Round up from the price you need, never down past it. Start from your cost, work out the price for your margin after the card fee, then move up to the next easy amount:

break-even = (cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee %)
price for a margin = (cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − margin %)
sign price = that price rounded up to the next $5

Break-even = (cost + 0.15) ÷ 0.974. Price for 30% margin = (cost + 0.15) ÷ 0.674. Sign = that price rounded up to the next $5. Fee = 2.6% of the sign price + 0.15. Keeps = sign − fee − cost. Margin = keeps ÷ sign.

Every sign price sits above the price for a 30% margin, so rounding up only adds margin. If all 50 sell, the stall takes $480.00, pays $19.98 in fees and $264.98 in materials, and keeps $195.04 (40.63%). Check: 480.00 − 19.98 − 264.98 = 195.04, and the lines add up the same way: 23.20 + 12.96 + 60.48 + 31.40 + 31.56 + 35.44 = 195.04.

How many signs? One tag per item would be 50 tags. One sign per type would be 6 signs. One sign per price is 4 signs: $5 for keychains and prints, $10 for earrings and ornaments, $15 for candles and $20 for mugs. Add one board listing all six types for the front of the table. With six types, the label vendor's "less than 10" rule says one big board on its own could also work.

Change: every sign price is a $5 step, so the change from a $20 note is always $15, $10, $5 or nothing. For cash sales at these prices you need no coins and no $1 notes; card sales need no change at all.

6. Making the signs: card sizes, stands and a sheet layout

Use large type: TheCraftMap suggests a "larger font than you think necessary". Write only the price and, if a sign covers two products, both names. Cards that stand at a slight angle are easier to read than cards lying flat.

If you print your own stands, measure the slot before you cut cards. In our pack, the price tag stand comes in three widths, 40, 60 and 90 mm, and its card slot stops short of each end, so the slot is shorter than the stand: 35, 55 and 85 mm. The M stand (60 mm wide) has a 55 mm slot, so use cards narrower than that, for example 50 × 80 mm. The slot is 1.0 mm wide on the S and M stands and 1.4 mm on the L. The stands have not been test-printed, so print one and try a card before you print a set.

Cards of 50 × 80 mm fit 4 across and 3 down, 12 to a sheet, on both US Letter (215.9 × 279.4 mm) and A4 (210 × 297 mm). The example's 4 signs plus a spare take 5 of them, so one sheet is enough.

Common questions

Should every item at a craft fair have a price tag? One-off pieces should. Products made in runs can share one sign per price, as long as every shopper can see a price without asking.

Should I use $9.99 or $10 at a craft fair? At a cash table, round amounts are faster to pay and need no coins. Whichever you pick, check the price still covers cost and fees, as in the table above. For sets and bundles, see how to price a gift set.

How big should price signs be? Big enough to read from the front of the table without leaning in. If in doubt, make the type larger.

How do I keep track of what sold? Give each price group its own line on a tally sheet; see how to keep track of sales at a craft show.
