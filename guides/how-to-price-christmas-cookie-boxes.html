How to price Christmas cookie boxes with mixed cookies

To price a Christmas cookie box with mixed cookies, cost each cookie type from its own batch, add up the cookies that go in the box, add the box, your packing time and overhead, then price for your margin after fees. In the worked example below, a standard box of 12 assorted cookies costs $21.13 and sells for $32.00, keeping $9.61 (30.03%) after an example fee. A small box of 6 sells for $17.00 and a tin of 18 for $49.00.

1. Cost each cookie from its own batch

A mixed box holds cookies from several batches, and each batch has its own cost. Cost every batch on its own: ingredients, oven and the hours it takes, divided by the cookies you can actually sell after breakage. The method, from pack prices to cost per gram, is in how to price cookies and baked goods; this guide starts from the result, the cost per cookie.

cost per cookie = (ingredients + oven + labour) ÷ sellable cookies

Decorated cookies take far longer per piece, so cost them as their own batch: see how to price decorated cookies per dozen.

2. Choose the mix and the box sizes

Keep the range small. Food-business software company Homegrown advises: "Start with 3 to 4 varieties." Three box sizes cover most gift buyers: a small bag or box of 6, a standard box of 12 and a larger tin of 18. Fix the mix for each size, for example 3 of each of four cookies in the box of 12, so every box of that size costs the same.

3. Add packaging and packing time per box

The box, tissue, ribbon and label are a cost per box, and so is the time it takes to fill, close and label it. Time a few boxes: a few minutes per box adds up over a holiday season. Add a small overhead allowance per box for things like trays, liners and card-reader costs.

box cost = cookies in the box + packaging + packing time + overhead

4. Price for your margin after fees

Most payment fees are a percentage plus a fixed amount per order. To keep your margin after the fee:

price = (box cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

Round the result up to a price you like. Rounding up only adds to what you keep.

5. Why the mix changes the price

Cookies that take longer per piece cost more, even when their ingredients are cheaper. In the example, gingerbread cut-outs have the cheapest ingredients after snickerdoodles but cost $1.60 each, because rolling and cutting take 2 hours a batch. Fill the same standard box with one cookie and the cost moves a lot:

  • 12 shortbread: 12 × $1.08 = $12.96, so the box costs $17.11.
  • 12 gingerbread: 12 × $1.60 = $19.20, so the box costs $23.35.

At one flat price of $35.00 (fee $1.35), the shortbread box keeps $16.54 and the gingerbread box $10.30. If you let buyers pick their own mix, price by the dearest cookie in it, or set a fixed mix per size as above.

6. Check common price ranges against your costs

Homegrown's guide for cookie sellers lists typical gift-set ranges. They are one vendor's figures, not market rates, but they are a useful check:

Our small and standard boxes fall inside the ranges; the tin of 18 is $1.00 above its top end, because the tin and the extra packing time cost money. Homegrown also says "a window box or tin adds $1 to $3 in cost but supports $5 to $10 in additional pricing" (Homegrown, 19 March 2026). Whether your buyers pay that much more is something only your own sales show, so start from your cost, not from the range.

A shortcut such as "ingredients × 3" leaves out your time. The example's standard box has $4.53 of ingredients (3 × $0.57 + 3 × $0.33 + 3 × $0.31 + 3 × $0.30), so the shortcut gives $13.59, which is below the box's $21.13 cost before any fee.

7. Before the holiday rush

Butter, chocolate and eggs change price often, so re-cost each batch before you take orders, and again if a key price moves. Decide how many boxes you can bake and pack in the hours you have, and when you stop taking orders: when to stop taking custom Christmas orders works back from the last shipping date. If you also offer a discount on several boxes, check it with how to price a gift set.
