How much to charge for Christmas mini sessions: price the whole day

Price a Christmas mini from the whole day, not the 15 minutes. Add the day's fixed costs (set, props, location, setup and marketing hours) to each family's own editing time, divide by the slots you expect to sell, then add the card fee each family pays. In the worked example a 12-slot day needs at least $104.50 a family when every slot sells, but $156.17 if only 6 do.

The formula

day cost = fixed hours × cost per hour + set, props, location and travel + families × (hours per family × cost per hour + gallery)
floor per family = (day cost ÷ families booked + fixed card fee) ÷ (1 − card fee %)

Your cost per hour comes from a year of costs (your pay, running costs, gear spread over its life) divided by the hours you work on clients; how to price a photography session works it out. Your pay is inside that hourly cost, so the floor already pays you; profit comes on top.

Step 1: the fixed cost of a mini day

Illustrative figures only; every input is invented. We use the same invented photographer as the session guide: $40.00 an hour, and a card fee of an assumed 2.9% + $0.30 per payment. Each family pays separately.

Some costs are the same whether you sell 4 slots or 12: building the set, props, the location fee, travel, and the hours spent marketing the day and setting up bookings. A gallery-software company's example weekend of minis lists "Props: $300", "Gas: $100", "Location fee: $100" and "Total cost to run your minis: $700" (mini session pricing article, updated 17 Oct 2024). Hours = 6.0 + 1.25 × families. Day cost = $602.00 + $51.00 × families. Floor = (day cost ÷ families + $0.30) ÷ 0.971 and price at 20% margin = (day cost ÷ families + $0.30) ÷ 0.771, both rounded up to the cent so the families' payments never fall short. Fee = price × 2.9%, rounded to the cent, + $0.30.

For 12 families: $1,214.00 ÷ 12 = $101.17 each. The floor is $104.50; the fee on it is $3.33, and $104.50 − $3.33 = $101.17, so each payment covers its share of the day exactly. With only 6 families the floor rises to $156.17. The article quoted above says it plainly: "If you don't book as many clients as expected, readjust your pricing accordingly!"

Bookings to break even = $602.00 ÷ (you keep − $51.00), rounded up to a whole booking. Profit = you keep × families − day cost. Margin = profit ÷ (price × families). Pay per hour worked = (you keep × 12 − $362.00 − $1.00 × 12) ÷ 21.00 hours; it includes the $40.00 an hour already in the cost.

At $150.00 you need 7 of the 12 slots before the day pays its costs and your time: with 7 booked, 7 × $145.35 = $1,017.45 against a day cost of $959.00, so $58.45 over. With 6 booked it loses $35.90. At $250.00 four bookings cover the day. That fill rate, not the price per minute, decides whether a mini day is worth running.

For comparison, one Florida studio lists its Christmas minis at "Investment: $250" with "10 professionally edited digital images included" (rate card), and the gallery-software article quoted above says it has "seen ranges of $250 to $800+" for holiday and themed minis. Those are one studio's and one company's figures, not market rates.

Step 5: no-shows and paying upfront

A photography tutorial site suggests: "require the session to be paid up-front to lessen the chance of no-show clients" (mini sessions article, updated 31 July 2026). In the example, at $250.00 with 12 booked, one unpaid no-show loses $242.45 kept and saves only $51.00 of editing and gallery, so profit falls by $191.45 to $1,503.95. The Florida studio above takes "A non-refundable $150 retainer is required to reserve your time slot." Whether a retainer is refundable depends on what you and the family agreed: check your agreement. Step 7: put a mini day into a pricing sheet

If your calculator has no per-family input, enter the mini day as one session: multiply per-family time and price yourself, then type the totals. In the 12-family example that is 12 × 0.25 = 3.0 hours of shooting, 12 × 0.75 = 9.0 hours of culling and editing, 12 × 0.25 + 3.0 = 6.0 hours of admin including marketing, 3.0 hours of travel and setup, and a price of 12 × $250.00 = $3,000.00. One difference: a sheet that sees one payment counts one $0.30 card fee, not twelve. At 2.9% + $0.30 that is $87.30 in fees against $90.60 for twelve separate $250.00 payments, so the real fees are $3.30 higher than the sheet shows.

Common questions

How much should I charge for Christmas mini sessions? At least the floor per family for the number of slots you expect to sell: the day cost divided by the families booked, plus the card fee. In the example that is $104.50 with all 12 slots sold.

How much do photographers charge for mini sessions? It varies widely: one Florida studio charges $250 for a Christmas mini, and one company's article quotes $250 to $800+ for holiday minis. Check any figure against your own floor first.

How many mini sessions should I book in a day? Enough to clear the break-even count at your price: 7 at $150.00 in the example, 4 at $250.00. More slots spread the fixed costs thinner, but only if they sell.

Should mini sessions be paid upfront? Taking payment or a retainer when the slot is booked lowers the risk of no-shows, as the tutorial site above suggests; in the example one unpaid no-show costs $191.45 of profit.

How many photos come with a mini session? The example uses 10 edited images, as the Florida rate card does. More images mean more editing hours per family, which raises the floor.
