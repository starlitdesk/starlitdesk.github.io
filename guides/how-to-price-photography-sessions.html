How to price a photography session: cost every hour, not the shoot

Price a photography session from the hours it really takes, not the hour you shoot. Add up a year of costs (your pay, running costs, gear spread over its life), divide by the hours you work on sessions to get a cost per hour, multiply by each session type's total hours including editing, then add the card fee. In the worked example below a 1.5-hour family shoot takes 6.5 hours and needs at least $283.52 just to cover costs and pay.

The formula

cost per session hour = a year of costs ÷ session hours a year
session cost = cost per session hour × total hours for the session + direct costs
break-even price = (session cost + fixed card fee) ÷ (1 − card fee %)

The break-even price already pays you, because your pay is part of the year of costs. Profit comes on top of it.

Step 1: a year of costs

Add three things for a full year:

  • Your pay: what you want to earn from photography in a year, before any profit for the business. For context only, the US median pay for photographers was $21.47 an hour in May 2025, and 67% of photographers were self-employed (US Bureau of Labor Statistics). That mixes employees and the self-employed, so it is not a price target.
  • Running costs: editing software, insurance, website, marketing, phone. Square suggests deciding a marketing budget for the year and dividing it over the projects you plan (Square).
  • Gear spread over its life: divide what each item cost by the years (or, as Square puts it, the projects) you expect it to last.

The idea is the same as in our guide on working out your hourly rate: the hours you spend on marketing, bookkeeping and learning still need paying, so they go into the cost, not into the hours you divide by.

Step 2: hours you really work per session

A "one-hour shoot" is never one hour of work. Count booking and emails, travel, the shoot, culling and editing, and delivery. One pricing article for photographers costs a portrait session sold as one hour at 385 minutes, counting booking, contracts, prep, travel, the shoot, post-production and a client meeting (Rangefinder). An editing-software vendor estimates 3-4 hours to edit a 50-image studio portrait session (Imagen, the vendor's own estimate). Time your next few sessions and use your own numbers.

Step 3: the break-even price, with the card fee built in

Illustrative figures only; every input is invented. A part-time portrait photographer: pay goal $40,000 a year; running costs $6,000; gear spread over its life: camera $2,400 ÷ 3 years = $800.00, lenses $3,000 ÷ 5 = $600.00, laptop $1,800 ÷ 3 = $600.00, so $2,000.00 a year. The card fee is an assumed 2.9% + $0.30 per payment; use your own.

A year of costs is $40,000 + $6,000 + $2,000.00 = $48,000.00. Of 1,500 working hours a year, 300 go to marketing, bookkeeping and learning, which leaves 1,200 session hours. Cost per session hour: $48,000.00 ÷ 1,200 = $40.00.

For the family session: 6.5 × $40.00 = $260.00, plus $15.00 direct costs = $275.00. Break-even is $275.30 ÷ 0.971 = $283.52; the fee on that is $8.52, and $283.52 − $8.52 = $275.00, so the price covers the cost and your pay exactly, with nothing left over.

Step 4: add a profit margin (margin vs markup)

A business needs a margin above break-even for slow months, new gear sooner than planned and growth. The table's last column builds a 20% margin into the price, after the card fee: (cost + $0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.029 − 0.20).

Watch the difference between markup and margin. Adding 50% to a $125.00 cost gives $187.50: that is a 50% markup, but the profit of $62.50 is only 62.50 ÷ 187.50 = 33.33% of the price. A 50% margin needs $125.00 ÷ (1 − 0.50) = $250.00. Say which one you mean when you set a target.

You keep = price − fee. Profit over cost = you keep − cost from the table above. Margin = profit ÷ price. Per shooting hour = price ÷ shoot hours; per hour worked = price ÷ total hours.

On the day, the mini session looks like $190.00 an hour. Counted over its two hours of work it is $47.50 an hour, and after its costs and your pay it clears only $8.94. Minis can still be worth running, for example to fill a quiet month, but price them from hours worked. If you run them as a holiday promotion, our Black Friday discount maths shows how far a discount can go before a session loses money.

How many sessions cover your year

At $360.00, each family session leaves $349.26 − $15.00 direct costs = $334.26 towards the year's $48,000.00. That takes $48,000.00 ÷ $334.26 = 143.60, so 144 sessions, about 12 a month, using 144 × 6.5 = 936 of the 1,200 session hours. If that number of bookings is not realistic where you work, the price, the hours per session or the costs need to change; a lower price makes the gap bigger.

Checking against local prices

Once you know your floor, look at what others charge. Square suggests researching about 10 competitors in your area and genre. Compare what is included (hours, edited images, prints), not just the headline price. If local prices sit below your break-even, compete on a shorter package with fewer images, not on a price below your floor.

Common questions

How much should I charge for my photography? At least the break-even price for each session type: a year of costs ÷ session hours × hours per session, plus direct costs and the card fee, then a margin on top.

Should I charge a deposit? That is your choice; our guide on how much deposit to take on a custom order shows the arithmetic of deposits and payment stages; whether a deposit is refundable depends on what you agreed, so check your agreement.

How often should I raise my prices? Whenever your yearly costs or your hours per session change: re-run the sum and compare.

What if clients say my prices are too high? Show the hours behind a session, or offer a shorter package. For changes clients ask for after the shoot, see how to price custom orders for extra time, revisions and rush fees.

Should I list my prices on my website? Either a "starting at" price or a full list works, as long as every listed price is above your floor.
