How to price handmade soap: cost per sellable bar, with trim, your time and selling fees

To price handmade soap, cost the whole batch, divide it by the bars you can sell after trim and rejects, add wrapping, your time and overhead, then price for your margin after fees: a cold process bar that costs $5.63 per sellable bar sells for $10.50 with an example 9.5% + $0.45 fee and keeps 33%. Many soap makers price a bar by adding up the oils and doubling it, or by matching what other makers charge. Both skip the costs that decide whether a bar makes money: lye and fragrance, the trim and rejects you cannot sell, wrapping and labels, your hours, and selling fees. This guide shows how to cost one sellable bar from a whole batch and price it so your margin survives the fees, with a worked example.

1. Cost the whole batch, not one bar

You buy oils, butters, lye and fragrance by the container and use part of each in a batch, so start from cost per gram (or per ounce):

cost per gram = pack price ÷ grams in the pack

Multiply by the weight your recipe uses and add up every line: oils and butters, lye, fragrance or essential oil, colour and additives, plus batch costs such as liner paper. Take the weights from your own recipe, which you have already run through a lye calculator. This guide costs a recipe; it does not check one.

2. Divide by the bars you can actually sell

A loaf that cuts into ten bars rarely gives ten sellable bars. Ends get trimmed, a bar cracks or sweats, one becomes your tester. Set a loss rate from your last few batches and divide by the sellable bars only:

sellable bars = bars cut × (1 − loss %)
material cost per bar = batch cost ÷ sellable bars

The bars you cannot sell were still paid for, so the good ones carry their cost.

3. Add your time, wrapping and overhead

Time a whole batch from weighing to cleanup, unmoulding and cutting, and divide by the sellable bars. Then add the per-bar work done later, such as wrapping and labelling. Pay yourself an hourly rate you would accept. Add the wrap and label per bar and a small allowance for overhead: moulds, gloves, scales, software.

Cold process bars also cure for weeks before you can sell them, so your money sits on a shelf for that time. The cost does not change, but it is one more reason not to price at break-even.

4. Price so your margin survives the fees

Selling fees are usually a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. To keep the margin you want after fees:

price = (cost per bar + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

With an example fee of 9.5% plus $0.45 per order and a 30% target margin: (5.63 + 0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.30) = $10.05. Rounded to $10.50, a single bar keeps $3.42 after fees and costs, a 33% margin, on top of paying you $18 an hour.

Now the "match the market" price of $7: fees take $1.12, costs $5.63, and $0.25 is left. Your time is still paid, but the bar earns the business almost nothing, and any slow week or price rise turns it into a loss.

Skipping the loss rate hides money too: dividing by all 10 bars gives $5.21 a bar, $0.42 less than the real cost. Across a few hundred bars a year, that gap is real.

5. Sets spread the order fee

The fixed fee is charged per order, not per bar. A set of three of the same bars costs $16.89; the same formula gives $28.66, so at $29 the set keeps $8.91, a 31% margin, while the buyer pays less than three single bars. Sets and gift boxes are often the best way to sell low-priced bars.

6. Check every soap and re-check often

Run the same calculation for each soap and sort by margin. Bars with expensive fragrance or butters, high loss, or fiddly decoration are often the ones below target. Re-check when oil or fragrance prices change and when your loss rate changes. In a spreadsheet, that takes minutes.
