How much to charge for shipping handmade items: the formula, the fee trap and free shipping

Charge what the parcel really costs you: carrier postage, plus packaging, plus the minutes it takes to pack at your hourly rate. On many marketplaces, selling fees also apply to the shipping you charge, so if you offer free shipping, add the full shipping charge to the item price: a $24 candle with $8.30 of shipping keeps $20.77 either way at $32.30, but only $13.30 if you ship free without changing the price. This guide shows how to work out the charge, when free shipping works, and how to handle orders with more than one item.

1. The shipping charge formula

A shipping charge has three parts. Postage is the obvious one. Packaging is easy to forget: boxes, mailers, tissue, filler, tape, labels and the thank-you card all count. Packing time is the one most makers leave out, even though printing a label, wrapping and boxing an order can take several minutes.

shipping cost = postage + packaging + packing minutes × your hourly rate ÷ 60

Craftybase describes the same formula (carrier postage plus packaging, plus a handling buffer) and works an example of $5.50 postage, $0.80 packaging and 5 minutes at $20 an hour, $7.97 in all (Craftybase). Use the hourly rate you price your making with; our hourly rate guide shows how to set one.

2. What drives postage: weight, distance and box size

Postage is rarely one flat number. Most carriers price by weight tier, by distance and, for large boxes, by size. In the US, for example, USPS Ground Advantage prices parcels under 1 lb in tiers of up to 4, 8, 12 and 15.999 oz, rounding up to the next tier, so a 4.2 oz parcel pays the 8 oz price. Heavier parcels are priced by the pound and by distance, and a box larger than 1 cubic foot is priced by dimensional weight (length × width × height ÷ 139, in inches and pounds) when that is more than its actual weight (USPS). Other carriers and countries use similar rules.

So weigh each product packed, not unpacked, and note which tier it falls in. A slightly smaller box or lighter filler can drop a parcel into a cheaper tier. If postage varies a lot by destination, base your charge on the zones you ship to most, or use the calculated shipping your selling platform offers.

3. Selling fees fall on shipping too

On many marketplaces and payment services, the percentage fee is charged on everything the buyer pays, shipping included. That has two effects. If you charge exactly your shipping cost, the fee on that amount comes out of your pocket. And if you offer free shipping by simply dropping the charge, you lose the whole shipping cost, not just the part you thought you were giving away. Check your own platform's fee terms to see what the percentage applies to.

Some guides add a margin on top of postage: Craftybase suggests 10 to 20% above the base carrier rate for most handmade products, as a rule of thumb (Craftybase). The worked example below keeps it simple and charges cost only, so you can see the fee effect on its own.

Because the fee falls on shipping as well, the free-shipping price that keeps the same take-home is simply the item price plus the shipping charge: $24.00 + $8.30 = $32.30. You can check it the other way round: ($20.77 + $8.30) ÷ (1 − 0.10) = $32.30.

4. When free shipping makes sense

Free shipping is a pricing choice, not a discount, as long as you build the cost into the item price as in option C. It suits light items where postage is a small share of the price; Craftybase suggests it works best for light, high-margin products with a carrier cost under about $5 (Craftybase). For heavy or bulky items, a separate charge keeps the item price comparable with local sellers.

Buyers notice shipping. In the Baymard Institute's survey of US online shoppers, extra costs that were too high (shipping, tax, fees) were the most common reason for leaving an order unfinished, given by 40% of respondents, excluding those who were just browsing (Baymard Institute). That is general online shopping, not handmade sellers only, but it is a reason to show the full price early, whichever way you charge.

5. Orders with more than one item

Charging the single-item rate for every item in a combined parcel overcharges the buyer. In the example, two candles in one box need $7.40 postage, $0.80 packaging and 8 minutes of packing ($2.67), so the parcel costs $10.87. Two single charges would be $16.60, $5.73 more than it costs to send. A fairer setup is a first-item rate of $8.30 and an additional-item rate of $2.57 ($10.87 − $8.30). Check whether your selling platform lets you set an additional-item rate.

6. Check your shipping once a season

Carrier prices change from time to time, and your packaging costs drift too. Re-weigh your best sellers packed, re-check the tiers and update your charges at least once a season. Before Christmas, also check the last shipping dates: our guide on when to stop taking custom Christmas orders works back from them, and how much holiday stock to make plans the busy weeks.

Common questions

How do I calculate shipping and handling fees for my handmade products? Add postage for the packed weight, the cost of all packaging, and your packing time at your hourly rate. That total is your shipping cost; charge at least that, and remember that fees may apply to it.

What is a handling fee and should I charge one? It is the packaging and packing-time part of the formula above. You do not need to show it as a separate line, but it should be in the shipping charge or in the item price.

Should I offer free shipping in my handmade shop? Only if you add the full shipping charge to the item price, as in option C. Then it is a presentation choice and costs you nothing extra per order.

How do I know if my shipping fees are hurting my sales? Compare how many visitors start to buy with how many finish, before and after a change to your shipping. Change one thing at a time and give it a few weeks.
