How to price jewelry when metal prices move: cost per piece, a re-price trigger and your fees

When silver or gold prices climb, jewelry makers face two questions: what does each piece really cost now, and is it time to change the price tag? Re-pricing on every move confuses buyers; never re-pricing lets the metal quietly eat your margin. This guide shows how to cost a piece from the metal you actually use, price it so your margin survives the fees, and work out in advance the metal price at which each piece needs a new price.

1. Cost the metal you use, not the metal you see

Start from your supplier's price per gram (or per ounce), and use the price you would pay to restock today, not what you paid months ago. Then weigh the metal that goes into one piece, and add your workshop loss: filings, sprue, offcuts and test pieces you do not recover. Track it over a few pieces; 5-10% is common for hand fabrication, but use your own figure.

metal cost = finished weight × (1 + loss %) × price per gram

2. Add findings, packaging, time and overhead

Add chain, findings (clasps, bails, jump rings, ear wires), stones or beads and packaging: box, card, pouch. Time each piece from cutting to polishing and pay yourself an hourly rate you would accept. Add a small overhead allowance for polishing compounds, saw blades, tools and software.

3. Price so your margin survives the fees

Selling fees are usually a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. To keep the margin you want after fees:

price = (cost per piece + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

4. Set a re-price trigger for each piece

Pick a floor: the lowest margin you will accept before you change a price (below your target, so small moves do not force a new tag). The money between what a piece keeps now and what it keeps at the floor is its buffer. Divide that buffer by the grams of metal the piece uses, and you know how far the metal price can rise before you must re-price:

trigger price per gram = today's price per gram + (keep now − floor % × price) ÷ grams used

Write the trigger next to each piece. When your supplier's price passes it, re-price that piece. Until then, leave it alone.

What a 10% rise does

If silver rises 10%, to $1.54 per gram, the pendant's cost goes up $0.76 and the cuff's $4.23. At the same prices, the pendant still keeps 29.5%, while the cuff drops to 26.4%. Same metal, same rise, very different effect: the cuff is mostly silver, the pendant is mostly time and chain.

5. When metal falls

Run the same numbers when prices fall. You can hold your prices and keep the extra margin as a buffer for the next rise, or lower the metal-heavy pieces. Whichever you choose, decide by the numbers, not by the headlines. This guide does not predict metal prices or advise on buying metal; it only helps you price what you make.

6. Re-check the whole range

List every piece with its metal, grams, cost, price and trigger, and sort by trigger. Your heaviest pieces in the dearest metal sit at the top: watch those. Gold-filled and plated pieces usually move less, because metal is a smaller share of their cost. In a spreadsheet, a new supplier price updates every piece at once.
