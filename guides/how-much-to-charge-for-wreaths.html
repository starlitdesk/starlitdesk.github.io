How much to charge for wreaths: greens, ring and your time

To work out how much to charge for a wreath, add up what it really costs you (the greens with some waste, the ring, wire, ribbon and decorations, and your time), then price it so your margin survives the payment fee. In the worked example below, a 24-inch fresh balsam door wreath costs $30.16 with 40 minutes of work, so the price floor at a 30% margin is $45.46. Priced at $48.00 it keeps $16.10; at a $29.95 farm-stand price the same wreath loses $1.41. Rules of thumb such as materials × 3 are a useful second opinion, but only your own cost shows whether a price pays you.

1. Cost the greens by weight or by bunch

Greens are bought by the bundle, the pound or the bunch, but a wreath uses only part of one. Divide what you paid by what you got, then multiply by what one wreath uses. Weigh the greens for a few wreaths rather than guessing: a fuller wreath can use far more than you think.

greens per wreath = bundle price ÷ bundle size × amount used

Then add waste. Some greens are too brown, too short or too bare to use, and trimming leaves a pile on the floor. Add a waste percentage to the greens only, because rings, wire and ribbon do not wilt. Count what you throw away over a few wreaths to find your own figure.

greens incl. waste = greens per wreath × (1 + waste %)

2. Add the ring, wire, ribbon and decorations

Everything else comes in packs: rings by the ten, wire by the spool, ribbon by the roll, cones and picks by the bag. Work out a cost per unit for each and multiply by what one wreath uses. A spool of wire that does eight wreaths costs one eighth of its price per wreath. These hardgoods are easy to forget because each is small, but together they can cost as much as the greens.

3. Pay yourself for the time

Time a wreath from the first cut to the bow, including cleaning the greens and tidying up. Multiply the minutes by an hourly rate you would accept for the work. If you are not sure what that rate is, see how to work out your hourly rate for handmade work.

labour = minutes ÷ 60 × hourly rate
total cost = greens incl. waste + hardgoods + labour

4. Price for your margin after fees

A payment fee, usually a percentage plus a fixed amount per sale, comes out of every price. Price so that what is left still covers your cost and your margin:

price floor = (total cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

The floor is the lowest price that keeps your target margin. You can charge more; charging less means the wreath pays you less than you planned.

5. Check the rules of thumb against your floor

Wreath makers share several quick rules. They are worth knowing, and worth checking against your own cost:

  • Materials × 3. howtomakewreaths.com's preferred rule is materials times three: it says "homemade wreaths that cost you $25 to make should retail for a minimum price of $75" (How to price handmade wreaths). For our wreath that is $16.83 × 3 = $50.49, above the $45.46 floor. But the rule does not see your time: if the same wreath took 60 minutes, labour would be $20.00, the total cost $36.83 and the floor ($36.83 + $0.30) ÷ 0.67 = $55.42, above the $50.49 the rule gives.
  • Costs including your wage × 2 = wholesale, × 2 again = retail. Made Urban gives the example formula "Costs (materials and your hourly wage) x 2 = wholesale price" and doubles that for retail (Made Urban, updated 3 March 2025). For our wreath: $30.16 × 2 = $60.32 wholesale and $120.64 retail. That builds in a large cushion; whether buyers in your area pay it is something only your own sales can show.

Made Urban also says "Handmade wreath prices tend to be over $50" in what it found online. Prices differ a lot by size, area and whether the wreath is fresh or dried, so treat any range as background, not as your price.

6. Selling to shops: check wholesale before you agree

Shops usually buy at wholesale prices, which Made Urban describes as "typically half the retail price". At half of $48.00 our wreath loses $7.18 (the table above). To keep a 30% margin on a wholesale order, with no payment fee on an invoice, the wholesale price must be at least $30.16 ÷ 0.70 = $43.09, so the retail price that matches it is about $86.18. If that is far above what your wreaths sell for, sell retail, or make a smaller wholesale design that costs less. More on this in wholesale vs retail pricing for handmade products.

7. Plan the season: greens, money and hours

Fresh wreaths sell in a short window: from about Thanksgiving (Thu 26 Nov 2026) and the first Sunday of Advent (Sun 29 Nov 2026) until Christmas. Work out the season before you order greens. For 20 of the example wreaths:

  • Greens: 20 × 4 lb = 80 lb, plus 15% waste = 92 lb, so 10 bundles of 10 lb ($180.00), with 8 lb spare.
  • Materials: 20 × $16.83 = $336.60 as costed per wreath (the spare greens are on top).
  • Time: 20 × 40 minutes = 800 minutes, or 13 hours 20 minutes.

Count those hours against everything else you make before the holidays: how much holiday stock to make shows how.

Common questions

How much does it cost to make a wreath?

Add the greens with waste, the ring, wire, ribbon and decorations, and your time. In our invented example, materials are $16.83 and the total with 40 minutes of work at $20.00 an hour is $30.16. Your figure depends on the size, how full you make it and what you pay for greens.

How much do handmade wreaths sell for?

It varies widely by size, area and materials. Start from your own price floor, then look at what similar wreaths near you sell for. If local prices sit below your floor, a smaller or simpler design may fit them better than a cut price.

Should a wreath class be priced the same way?

The same steps apply per seat: the materials for each wreath, a share of the room and your teaching time spread over the seats. This guide does not cover classes in detail.

Do dried or faux wreaths need waste?

Usually less: dried and faux materials do not wilt, but some still break or shed. Use your own records for the rate, or leave waste at zero if you throw nothing away.
