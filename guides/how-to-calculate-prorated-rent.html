How to calculate prorated rent for a partial month

To prorate rent, find a daily rate and multiply it by the days the tenant has the unit. The daily rate is the monthly rent divided by the days in that month, by 30, or (rent × 12) ÷ 365; your lease and local rules decide which one you use. Round once, at the end. In the example, $1,150.00 rent and a move-in on Fri 16 Oct give $593.55, $604.93 or $613.33 depending on the method, a $19.78 spread.

1. What prorated rent is

Rent is usually due for a whole month. When a tenant moves in or out partway through a month, you charge only for the days they have the unit. An online learning site puts it simply: "Prorated rent is what a renter pays for a partial month of rent" (online learning site, last updated 29 Oct 2021). The arithmetic is the same every time:

prorated rent = daily rate × days occupied

Count the days the same way every time. In this guide both the move-in day and the move-out day count as occupied days; that is a choice your lease may make differently.

2. Three ways to get a daily rate

A landlord software company's guide lists three methods (landlord software guide, 4 Dec 2025):

  • Days in that month: rent ÷ 28, 29, 30 or 31. The guide says: "This is the most common method for residential rentals."
  • A 30-day month: rent ÷ 30, whatever the month. The same guide says it "has now been mostly replaced by the method above."
  • Days in the year: rent × 12 ÷ 365 (366 in a leap year), so the daily rate is the same all year.

None of these is right everywhere. The same guide adds: "Laws and regulations for landlords vary by state and locality". This guide does not say which method applies where you are; check your lease and the rules where the property is.

3. Worked example: move-in on Fri 16 Oct

Invented figures, labelled illustrative: unit A-1 from our rent guide, rent $1,150.00 a month, due on the 1st. The tenant moves in on Fri 16 Oct 2026 and out on Fri 12 Feb 2027. Days include the move-in day and the move-out day.

October has 31 days, so a move-in on the 16th covers 16 days (16 to 31). February 2027 has 28 days, so a move-out on the 12th covers 12 days (1 to 12).

Daily rates are shown rounded to the cent. Each amount is worked out unrounded and rounded once: 1150 ÷ 31 × 16 = 593.55; 1150 × 12 ÷ 365 × 16 = 604.93; 1150 ÷ 30 × 16 = 613.33; 1150 ÷ 28 × 12 = 492.86; 1150 × 12 ÷ 365 × 12 = 453.70; 1150 ÷ 30 × 12 = 460.00.

For the move-in month the three methods differ by 613.33 − 593.55 = $19.78, and the days-in-month method is the lowest. If you also collect November in full at move-in, the first payment is 593.55 + 1,150.00 = $1,743.55 (deposits are outside this guide).

4. Move-out in February: why the cheapest method changes

In February the order flips. With only 28 days, the days-in-month method gives the highest daily rate, $41.07, and the highest amount: 492.86 − 453.70 = $39.16 more than the days-in-the-year method. The same 12 days in a 31-day month such as March would be 1150 ÷ 31 × 12 = $445.16, so $47.70 less than in February.

So no method is always cheaper for the tenant or always better for the landlord. Pick the one your lease names, write it down, and use it for every move-in and move-out. Over the whole example lease, with the days-in-month method: 593.55 + 3 × 1,150.00 + 492.86 = $4,536.41.

Leap years: February 2028 has 29 days (Tue 29 Feb 2028), so the days-in-month method divides by 29 that year, and the days-in-the-year method by 366.

5. Round once, at the end

Keep the daily rate unrounded and round the final amount to the cent. Rounding the daily rate first adds small errors: in the example, 37.10 × 16 = 593.60, which is $0.05 more than 593.55. A rent collection software guide shows the same effect: it gives "$1,400 / 30 days = $46.67 daily rent rate" and then "$46.67 x 10 days occupied = $466.67" (rent collection software guide, last updated 6 Sep 2024). Worked from the rounded rate, 46.67 × 10 is $466.70; worked unrounded, 1400 ÷ 30 × 10 is $466.67. Pick one way and use it every time; rounding once at the end is the simpler rule.

6. Recording it: the prorated amount goes on that month's row

In a rent ledger with one row per unit per month, the prorated amount is the rent due on that month's row: $593.55 on A-1's October row and $492.86 on its February row, with the full $1,150.00 on the months in between. Then status and balance work as usual: if the tenant pays $593.55 for October, that row is paid in full and the balance is $0.00. Note the method and the days in the row's note, for example "prorated, 16 of 31 days".

Common questions

How do you calculate prorated rent for a move-in? Daily rate × days from the move-in day to the end of the month. In the example, 1150 ÷ 31 × 16 = $593.55 with the days-in-month method.

How do you calculate prorated rent for a move-out? Daily rate × days from the 1st to the move-out day. In the example, 1150 ÷ 28 × 12 = $492.86 for 12 days of February.

How do you calculate prorated rent for February? With the days-in-month method, divide by 28, or 29 in a leap year. With the other two methods February is no different from any month, which is why the three methods are furthest apart in February.

Which method is correct? The one your lease and local rules call for. This guide shows the arithmetic for all three; it does not say which one applies where you are.
