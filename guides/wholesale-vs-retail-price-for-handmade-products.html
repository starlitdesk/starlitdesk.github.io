Wholesale vs retail price for handmade products: the four-times test and what you keep per channel

Wholesale is usually half of retail: a shop buys at your wholesale price and doubles it, so your retail price needs to be at least four times what an item costs you to make. Work out the full cost (materials, your time and overhead), double it for wholesale, double that for retail, then check what you actually keep in each channel. In the worked example below, a candle that costs $9.20 sells wholesale at $18.40 and retail at $36.80, and the maker keeps anywhere from $4.60 to $23.65 per candle depending on where it sells.

1. Start from the full cost of one item

Wholesale only works if the wholesale price covers everything it costs you to make the item, because the shop takes the rest. Count all three parts:

cost = materials + labour + overhead

Labour is your timed making minutes at an hourly rate you would accept; our guide on working out your hourly rate shows how to set it. Overhead covers tools, studio costs, software and the like. One pricing guide suggests 10-15% of labour plus materials as a starting point (Craft Professional); use your own figure if you have it.

2. Double for wholesale, double again for retail

The most common rule in handmade pricing guides is:

wholesale price = cost × 2
retail price = wholesale price × 2

Shops call the second step keystone pricing: they buy at half the retail price and sell at full retail (Craftybase, Craft Professional). The shop's half pays for its rent, staff and unsold stock, so most shops will not take your work at a smaller discount.

Keep your own retail price at the same level as the shops' price. If you sell online for much less than the shop on the high street, the shop is unlikely to reorder.

3. The four-times test

A quick check before you approach any shop:

retail price ÷ 4 ≥ full cost per item

If your retail price divided by four is above your full cost, keystone wholesale can work (Craftybase). If it is below, you have three choices: raise your retail price, lower your cost, or keep that product out of wholesale.

4. Compare what you keep in each channel

The same item can earn very different amounts depending on where it sells:

  • Direct wholesale: a shop orders from you and pays your invoice. You get the full wholesale price, before any payment or invoicing costs.
  • A wholesale marketplace: shops order through a platform that takes a commission from you. Rates vary; as an example, one large wholesale marketplace publishes a higher commission on a new shop's first order than on reorders.
  • Consignment: the shop displays your stock and pays you a share only when an item sells. A 60/40 split (60% to you) is often called typical; some makers negotiate 70/30 and some shops offer 50/50 (Craft Professional).
  • Your own retail: you sell at full retail and pay your own selling and payment fees.

Worked example: one candle, four channels

Illustrative figures only: the marketplace commission (25% on a first order, 15% on a reorder) and the retail fee (9.5% + $0.45 per order) are example rates, not any platform's quote. Use your own costs and the rates you are actually offered.

Wholesale is $9.20 × 2 = $18.40, and retail is $18.40 × 2 = $36.80. The four-times test passes: $36.80 ÷ 4 = $9.20, exactly the full cost.

Kept after cost = received − $9.20. The table leaves out shipping, packaging for transport and payment terms, which differ by shop.

Your own retail keeps the most per candle, but you find every buyer yourself. Direct wholesale keeps exactly the $9.20 you built in as profit, and one order moves many units at once: a $150 opening minimum, a common figure for first orders (Craftybase), is 9 candles, or $165.60. The marketplace's first-order commission halves what you keep, which is why reorders and the shops you bring yourself matter so much on those platforms.

Consignment looks better than wholesale per unit ($12.88 against $9.20), at 70/30 it is $16.56, and at 50/50 it falls back to $9.20. But you are paid only when items sell, the stock is yours until then, and anything damaged or unsold comes back to you. Weigh the higher share against the wait and the risk.

5. When the numbers do not fit

Suppose shops in your area sell candles like this one at $32. Then $32 ÷ 4 = $8.00, and the $9.20 candle fails the four-times test. Either raise the retail price, or bring the cost down: batching wicking and labelling to cut labour to 12 minutes ($4.00) and buying wax in bulk to cut materials to $2.80 gives a full cost of $2.80 + $4.00 + $1.02 overhead = $7.82, which passes. Never cut your own pay to make wholesale fit; that only hides the loss.

6. Set your terms before the first order

Decide your opening minimum, your reorder minimum, who pays shipping, when invoices are due, and for consignment the split, the payout dates and who pays for lost or damaged stock. Write them on a simple line sheet with your wholesale prices and send it to every shop. Clear terms make a small maker look professional and save awkward conversations later.

7. Track each shop separately

Once you have more than one stockist, keep a record per shop: what you delivered, what sold, what came back, what each shop owes you and which invoices are late. After a few months, rank shops by profit, not by order size, and focus on the ones that reorder and pay on time.
