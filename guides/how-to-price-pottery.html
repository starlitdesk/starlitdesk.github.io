How to price pottery: kiln firing cost per piece, cracked pieces, your time and fees

To price pottery, add clay, glaze and each piece's share of two kiln firings by shelf space, spread cracks and seconds over the pieces you sell, add your time, packaging and overhead, then price for your margin after fees: a mug that costs $10.47 sells for $19 with an example 9.5% + $0.45 fee and keeps 33%. Most potters price a mug from the clay, the glaze and their time. Two costs often go missing: the electricity for two kiln firings, and the pieces that crack, warp or come out as seconds. Each is small on its own, but together they can take a few points off your margin, and a half-empty kiln or a bad firing takes more. This guide shows how to cost one sellable piece, including firing and losses, and price it so your margin survives the fees.

1. Cost a kiln load, then split it

An electric kiln's energy use is roughly its rated power times the firing hours times its average draw. Elements switch on and off as the kiln follows its schedule, so it rarely draws full power for the whole firing: the average draw is your own estimate, and your electricity meter or kiln log is the best source. Multiply by your price per kWh to get the cost of one load. Do it separately for bisque and glaze firings, because the hours and the number of pieces that fit differ.

load cost = kW × hours × average draw % × price per kWh
firing cost per piece = bisque load ÷ pieces per bisque load + glaze load ÷ pieces per glaze load

Fire at a shared studio? Use the studio's firing fee per piece instead of the kiln maths.

2. Give big pieces their kiln space

A serving bowl can take the shelf space of two mugs. If it does, it should carry two mugs' worth of firing cost. Count pieces per load in "mug spaces" and charge each piece for the spaces it uses.

3. Spread cracks and seconds over the good pieces

If one piece in ten cracks, warps or ends up a second you will not sell at full price, the nine good ones have to pay for the clay, glaze and firing of the tenth. Track your loss rate over a few firings for each kind of piece.

cost per sellable piece = (clay + glaze + other + firing) ÷ (1 − loss %)

4. Add time, packaging and overhead, then price for fees

Add your making time at an hourly rate you would accept (wedging, throwing, trimming, handles, glazing), packaging, and a small overhead allowance for tools, kiln shelves, elements and software. Selling fees are usually a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. To keep the margin you want after fees:

price = (cost per piece + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

Worked example: a mug and a serving bowl

Illustrative figures only, not measured values: an 11.5 kW kiln, 9-hour bisque and 11-hour glaze firings, a 50% average draw, electricity at $0.17 per kWh, 30 mug spaces per bisque load and 24 per glaze load, clay at $22.00 per 10 kg bag. Use your own kiln, prices and loss rates.

A bisque load costs $8.80 and a glaze load $10.75. Per mug space that is $0.29 + $0.45 = $0.74 of firing.

What leaving out firing and loss does

Cost the same pieces from clay, glaze, time, packaging and overhead only, and the formula suggests $16.31 for the mug and $23.60 for the bowl. Priced at $17 and $24, they keep $4.47 (26%) and $5.47 (23%), not 30%. The bowl loses more because it takes two spaces in every firing.

A half-empty kiln or a bad firing

A load costs about the same to fire whether the kiln is full or not. Fire the glaze load with 12 mugs instead of 24 and the mug's firing cost rises from $0.74 to $1.19. Have a bad month with 20% losses as well, and the mug costs $11.41.

The full-cost price absorbs a bad firing and stays near target. The price that left out firing and loss starts below target and falls further. Filling the kiln and bringing the loss rate down are worth as much as a price change.

5. Re-check when something changes

Re-run the numbers when your electricity tariff, clay price, firing schedule or loss rate changes, and when you start making a new shape. A shape with a high loss rate or one that takes a lot of shelf space is where a price review pays off first.
