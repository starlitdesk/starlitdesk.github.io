How much to charge for melt and pour soap

Work out what one bar really costs: the base by the ounce, fragrance and colour per batch, wrapping, your time and a share of overhead. Then price so a target margin is left after your selling fees. In our example a 4 oz holiday bar costs $5.29 to make and needs about $9.50; three times the materials alone ($7.02) would leave 61 cents. Melt and pour feels quick and cheap, so it is easy to underprice; this guide adds up the costs that are easy to miss, with a worked example.

1. Cost the base by the ounce, not by the block

Base is sold by the pound or by the block, and you use part of it in each batch. Turn the price, including shipping, into a cost per ounce:

cost per ounce = (block price + shipping) ÷ ounces in the block

One supplier lists a 10 lb block of basic clear melt and pour base "From $65.13" (Wholesale Supplies Plus, read Sun 11 Oct 2026; supplier prices change often). Another supplier estimates "16 oz of melt and pour soap base will cost you around $6.99 depending on what base you choose" (Bramble Berry). Use your own price and the weight you actually melt, including what stays in the jug.

Illustrative figures only; every input in this guide's example is invented apart from the block price above. Use your own.

In the example: $65.13 + $14.00 shipping = $79.13 for 160 oz, so $79.13 ÷ 160 = $0.4945625 an ounce. A batch melts 33 oz (1 oz stays in the jug): 33 × 0.4945625 = $16.32, plus $2.40 fragrance and colour = $18.72 a batch.

2. Add fragrance, colour and wrap per batch

Add what the batch uses of fragrance, colour and any additives as one batch cost, from your own recipe; this guide gives no usage rates. Then add packaging per bar: a wrap, a label, a band.

3. Count your time, even for "quick" soap

Melt and pour spares you handling lye, but melting, colouring, pouring, unmoulding, cleaning up and wrapping still take time. Time a whole batch and divide by the bars it makes, then add the per-bar wrapping time. Pay yourself an hourly rate you would accept; how to work out your hourly rate shows how to set one.

4. Price so your margin survives the fees

Selling fees are usually a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. To keep the margin you want after fees:

price floor = (cost per bar + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

A soap cost calculator from a software vendor says melt and pour works the same way as other soap, "the inputs and formula are identical", and calls a 3x markup on cost "the standard recommendation" (Craftybase, Mar 2026; a vendor rule of thumb). The catch is what you multiply: section 6 shows why.

The minis keep a little more of their price, but each one is a separate sale with its own order fee unless the buyer takes several.

6. Why "3x materials" undercharges melt and pour

A markup only works on the full cost. Multiply the materials alone and your time, wrapping and the fees are left out: 3 × $2.34 = $7.02. Fees $1.12, cost $5.29: 7.02 − 1.12 − 5.29 = $0.61, 8.69% of the price. Your time is paid inside the cost, but the business keeps almost nothing. Three times the full cost would be $15.87, well above the floor.

The same supplier says that, looking at small-batch artisan soap makers, it sees prices "from $8.00 to $15.00 and more per 4 oz bar" (Bramble Berry). The example's $9.50 sits inside that range; check where your own floor sits before you match anyone's price. One maker on a forum even found their melt and pour bars cost more to make than their cold process ones (Soap Making Forum, 2019; one maker's experience).

8. December timing: melt and pour vs cold process

Melt and pour "hardens in several hours, and doesn't need to cure", while "cold process soap takes about 4-6 weeks to cure" (Bramble Berry). That makes melt and pour the soap you can still make late in the season. For a market on Fri 11 Dec, cold process bars must be poured by Fri 13 Nov for a 4-week cure, or by Fri 30 Oct for 6 weeks; melt and pour poured the same week can still be wrapped and sold. Christmas Day is Fri 25 Dec. To plan quantities, see how much holiday stock to make; for made-to-order soap, when to stop taking custom Christmas orders. Common questions

How much to charge for melt and pour soap? At least your floor: cost per bar plus the fixed fee, divided by what is left after the fee % and your target margin. In the example that is $9.49 for a 4 oz bar.

How much to charge for a bar of homemade soap? The same method works for any bar; cold process adds lye, trim and cure time. See how to price handmade soap.

How much to charge for handmade soap? Start from the full cost, not from other makers' prices, then compare: if your floor sits above the going rate, look at your costs, your bar size or a set.

Do I need a license to sell melt and pour soap? Rules depend on where you sell; check with your local authority. How a soap is labelled can depend on what the base is made of, so ask your supplier what the base is (Soap Queen).
