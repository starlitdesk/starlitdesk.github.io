What to keep track of in a handmade business, beyond sales

Beyond sales and expenses, a small handmade business needs four records: what each product costs and keeps at its price, what each event made per hour, how many hours your open orders need before the ship-by date, and what each shop owes you or still holds. In the illustrative month below the bank shows +$735.00, but the records show $466.50 more owed or sitting in shops, a cost rise that took $60.00, a fair that paid $5.12 an hour, and two custom orders that will not fit before Christmas.

1. Why a bank balance is not enough

Your bank balance shows money in and money out. It does not show what you earned but have not been paid yet, stock that is sitting in someone else's shop, a product whose materials went up while its price stayed the same, or work you have promised but not made. A maker selling at markets once put the question plainly: "I know I need to keep records, but I am a bit confused as to what I need to keep track of" (Flying Solo forum, 2010). This guide answers it with four small records and one worked month.

2. Sales and expenses: the base record

Every business needs a record of what came in and what went out. One craft inventory software company suggests recording income by channel with the date, products, quantities, discounts and "shipping charged versus actual cost", and material purchases with quantity, unit cost, supplier and arrival date (Craftybase, updated April 2026). For a monthly view of profit by channel, see is my craft business making a profit? What you must keep for tax, and for how long, depends on your country: check with your tax office.

The four records below sit on top of that base. Each one catches something the bank balance hides.

3. Record 1: product costs and what each price keeps

For each product, write down the cost of one piece (materials, packaging and anything else you buy for it), the fee at your price, and what you keep. When a supplier raises a price, update the cost the same day: the margin falls quietly while the price stays put. For the full method, see how to price handmade items.

4. Record 2: each market or fair, per hour

For each event, record sales, the booth fee, the cost of the items sold, card fees and every hour it took, including setup and travel. Profit per hour is the number that lets you compare events. One craft-business blog keeps an "Event/Channel" column in its sales log ("Which fair, market, or shop") so totals per event come straight out of the sheet (TheCraftMap, June 2026). To decide whether to book a fair again, see is a craft fair worth it?

5. Record 3: open orders against the hours you have

List each open custom order with the hours it still needs, add them up, and compare the total with the making hours you have before your last ship-by date. If the orders need more hours than you have, you know now, not in mid-December. See when to stop taking custom Christmas orders. To set up the list itself, see how to keep track of orders.

6. Record 4: stockists, stock out and money owed

For each shop that sells your work, record what you left or invoiced, what has sold, your share, what has been paid and what is still owed, plus the pieces still on its shelves. For the details, see how to keep track of consignment stock and how to track net 30 invoices.

Materials for Product A went up by $1.50 a piece. At the old price, 40 sold in November kept $60.00 less than before. One option, not a rule: the price that holds the old 41.33% margin is (9.90 + 0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.4133) = $21.05, rounded up to the next 50 cents, $21.50.

Record 3: open orders against hours left

12 open custom orders × 3.5 hours = 42.00 hours. From Sunday 22 November to Thursday 17 December is 25 days; at 10 making hours a week that is 25 ÷ 7 × 10 = 35.71 hours. The orders need 6.29 hours more than you have: 10 orders fit (35.71 ÷ 3.5 = 10.2), and 2 need a later date or a polite no.

The 10 making hours a week and 3.5 hours per order are invented. The date is the 2026 USPS Ground Advantage last recommended send date for the contiguous US (USPS); use your own carrier's date and leave time to pack.

None of this is visible in the bank balance, and every line changes a decision: re-price Product A, think twice about Fair 2, say no or a later date to two orders, and chase Shop B on Sunday 29 November.

8. How often to update each one

TheCraftMap suggests "Fifteen minutes every Sunday" to log a week's sales and material purchases, and a stock count once every three months. As a rule of thumb (our extension of that figure, not theirs), four small records at 15 minutes each come to about an hour a week:

  • Product costs: whenever a supplier price changes, and once a month.
  • Events: the day after each event, while you remember the hours.
  • Open orders: each time you take or finish an order, and weekly in the busy season.
  • Stockists: when you drop off stock, send an invoice or get paid, and on each due date.

9. One big tracker or four small ones?

You do not need one giant tracker. Four small records, each kept where you use it, are easier to keep current than one sheet that tries to do everything. A spreadsheet is usually enough for a small shop. Craftybase, which sells inventory software, suggests looking at dedicated software when two or more signs apply, such as more than 20 unique products, more than 50 orders a month or 15 or more different materials (Craftybase spreadsheet guide, updated April 2026); TheCraftMap puts the limit for a single inventory sheet at more than 100 products. Those are vendor rules of thumb, not hard limits.

Common questions

What should a small handmade business keep track of? Sales and expenses, plus four records: product costs and what each price keeps, results per event per hour, open orders against the hours you have, and what each shop owes you or still holds.

How do I keep track of inventory in a small business? Track stock in three places: at home, at events and in shops. Record 4 covers stock in shops; count everything you hold once a quarter and correct the sheet.

Do I need inventory software, or is a spreadsheet enough? Below the rough limits in section 9, a spreadsheet is usually enough.

How often should I update my records? Weekly, about 15 minutes per record, plus a stock count every three months.

What records do I need to keep for tax? That depends on your country; check with your tax office. This guide covers business records for decisions, not tax rules.
