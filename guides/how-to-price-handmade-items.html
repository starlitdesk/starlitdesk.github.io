How to price handmade items: a simple formula that includes your time and selling fees

A simple formula for pricing handmade items: add up materials, your time at an hourly rate, packaging and a share of overhead to get your cost per item, then divide by what is left after selling fees and the margin you want to keep: price = (cost + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − margin %). A pouch that costs $18.05 to make sells for $31 with an example 9.5% + $0.45 fee and still keeps 31%. This guide explains each part, compares the result with popular shortcuts, and links to detailed guides for ten crafts.

1. Materials: cost what goes into one item

Price every material from the pack you bought: a metre of fabric, a box of 100 findings, a reel of thread. Divide by how much one item uses. Small things such as glue, thread and labels add up; give them a small allowance if measuring is impractical. Use what it would cost to restock today, not what you paid last year.

2. Your time: pick an hourly rate and time the work

Your time is a cost even if no one pays you a wage. Choose an hourly rate you would accept for the work, then time a few items from start to finish, including cutting, finishing and packing. Estimates are usually low; a timer is not.

Setting the rate. Decide what you want to earn from making each month and divide by the hours you actually spend making. Leave out admin, photos and packing: your margin pays for those. In Craftybase's example, $1,800 a month ÷ 80 making hours = $22.50 an hour, which it treats as a minimum. For a yearly version with a worked example, see how to work out your hourly rate.

3. Packaging and overhead

Add the box, bag, tissue or card that leaves with each item. Then add a small amount per item for overhead: a share of tools, equipment wear, software, photography and market fees that every item has to carry.

Your share of overhead. Add up the monthly costs that belong to no single item (software, studio, packaging stock, tools spread over a year) and divide by the items you expect to sell that month. In the same Craftybase example, $120 of monthly overhead over about 200 candles a month is $0.60 a candle. Dividing by items sold rather than items made is the safer choice while stock is still building up.

cost per item = materials + time × hourly rate + packaging + overhead

4. Price for the margin you keep after fees

Selling and payment fees are usually a percentage of the sale plus a fixed amount per order. If you only add a markup to your cost, the fees quietly eat part of it. Set the margin you want to keep after fees instead:

price = (cost per item + fixed fee per order) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin %)

Use the rates your own platform and payment provider publish, and check them a few times a year. The lowest price that only covers your costs and fees is your break-even: (cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee %).

With an example fee of 9.5% plus $0.45 per order and a 30% target margin: (18.05 + 0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.30) = $30.58. Rounded up to $31, the fees are $3.40 and the pouch keeps $9.55 (31%) after fees and costs, on top of paying you $18 an hour. Its break-even price is $20.44.

5. How the popular shortcuts compare

Materials × 3. Materials are $4.95, so the price would be $14.85. After fees and the full cost, each pouch loses $5.06. Even if you count your time as free, it leaves $6.94 for 40 minutes of work: $10.41 an hour. For handmade work, time is usually the biggest cost, and a materials multiplier cannot see it.

(Materials + labour + overhead) × 2 for wholesale, × 2 again for retail. This classic formula gives $34.90 wholesale and $69.80 retail. It is generous: at $69.80 the pouch would keep $44.67. Whether buyers pay that is a market question, not a costing one. It is a useful ceiling and a reminder of what wholesale needs.

The wholesale check. Shops usually expect to buy at about half the retail price. Half of $31 is $15.50, less than the $18.05 the pouch costs. So $31 works for direct sales, but this pouch cannot be sold wholesale at that retail price without a loss. If you plan to sell to shops, price retail at least twice your wholesale price, and make sure wholesale covers your cost.

6. Check every product, not just one

Run the same calculation for every product and sort them by margin. Items below break-even are the first to re-price, re-design (less time, cheaper materials) or drop. Re-check when material prices or fees change.

Common questions

How do I calculate my hourly rate for handmade items? Divide the pay you want from making by the hours you spend making, not by all your working hours. The hourly rate guide works through a full year.

What is a fair markup for handmade items? A common rule of thumb is 2 to 3 times your cost. This guide works the other way round: it sets the price so the margin you choose is still there after fees, which a fixed markup cannot promise.

How do I price handmade items for wholesale? Shops usually buy at about half the retail price, so wholesale must cover your full cost and retail must be at least twice wholesale. The wholesale check in section 5 shows how to test a price, and our guide to wholesale vs retail pricing compares wholesale, consignment and retail side by side.

How often should I reprice handmade items? Whenever a main material, your time per item or your fees change, and at least once a year. The jewelry guide shows a re-price trigger for a material whose price moves often.
